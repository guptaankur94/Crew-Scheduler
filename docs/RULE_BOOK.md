# CrewCircadian rule book

Every piece of advice in the app comes from one of these rules. This file is generated from the code (`npm run rulebook`), so it always matches what the app says. **Status: awaiting review by a sleep physician.**

Evidence grades: **strong** (consistent trials or reviews), **moderate** (some trials or expert guidance), **limited** (early or mixed; shown as a gentle guide), **practical** (arithmetic on the roster and settings), **regulation** (a legal rule, not a health claim).

## Numbers the engine uses

- Caffeine cut-off before sleep, by usual amount: one coffee or a few chai 6 h, two coffees 8 h, three or more / energy drinks 12 h; plus 2 h before a daytime sleep (our margin).
- Hard exercise: finish 4 h before a night's sleep (not shown before sleep after a night duty).
- Alcohol: none in the 12 h before check-in or a home standby.

| ALC-01 | regulation | DGCA rules on alcohol for crew (12 hours before flight duty) | Crew must not drink alcohol in the 12 hours before a flight duty, and may be breath-tested before flying. | Even a couple of drinks also reduce deep and dream sleep (Gardiner et al. 2025). Your airline may set a longer limit; follow whichever is stricter. | Plan the last drink at least 12 hours before check-in, or before your standby starts. | [source](https://www.teamleaseregtech.com/updates/article/26621/dgca-notified-the-procedure-for-medical-examination-of-aircraft-person/) |
| ALERT-01 | moderate | Fatigue Management Guide for Airline Operators (2015) | For people adjusted to their home time, alertness is usually lowest in the early hours, roughly 02:00–06:00. | This window is an average across people. Yours may be earlier or later, and it moves after travel or night work. | Move around when duties allow, drink water, and cross-check tasks with your crew. | [source](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf) |
| CAF-01 | moderate | Gardiner et al. 2025: caffeine dose, timing and sleep | About one cup of coffee (around 100 mg) up to about 4 hours before bed had little effect on sleep; a large dose (400 mg) should stop about 12 hours before. | Your cut-off follows how much caffeine you usually have (Settings): 6 hours for one coffee or a few cups of chai, 8 for two coffees, 12 for more. Caffeine disturbs daytime sleep after a night duty more than night sleep, so before a daytime sleep the app adds 2 hours; that margin is our own choice, as research has not pinned down the exact amount. Daily limits: 400 mg, or 200 mg in pregnancy (EFSA). | Have coffee early in a duty rather than near its end, and stop earlier before daytime sleep. | [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985402/) |
| EXER-01 | moderate | Leota et al. 2025: evening exercise and sleep across 4 million nights | Hard exercise that ended within about 4 hours of bedtime was linked to later, shorter and poorer sleep. Lighter exercise had little effect. | This is a link seen in wearable data, not a trial, so treat it as a sensible guide. After a night duty, sleep comes first; keep movement gentle. | Do hard workouts earlier in the day. A walk or stretching in the evening is fine. | [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/) |
| LIGHT-01 | moderate | Brown et al. 2022: expert recommendations on daytime and evening light | Bright light in the daytime and dim light in the evening help keep your body clock steady. | Daylight is far brighter than indoor lighting, so time outdoors in the morning is an easy way to get it. | Spend some time in daylight in the morning, and keep lights dim in the evening before sleep. | [source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35298459) |
| LIGHT-02 | moderate | Crowley et al. 2003: bright light at work and dark sunglasses on the way home | Night workers who wore dark sunglasses on the way home in the morning adapted better to their sleep schedule. | Bright morning light tells your body clock it is daytime, which can make it harder to sleep when you get home. | Wear dark sunglasses from the airport until you are in a darkened room. | [source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14667152) |
| MEAL-01 | limited | Liu et al. 2022 (New England Journal of Medicine): time-restricted eating trial | Eating mostly in the daytime is reasonable, but large trials find strict eating windows no better for weight than simply eating less. | Treat this window as a gentle guide, not a rule. Do not fast through a duty to stay inside it. | Have balanced meals with protein and vegetables when you can, and keep water with you. | [source](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833) |
| MEAL-02 | moderate | Centofanti et al. 2024: meal versus snack during simulated night work | A full meal in the middle of the night worsened next-day blood-sugar control more than a small snack did. | Your body handles food less well during its biological night. A small snack avoids most of the effect. | Have your main meal before a night duty. If you get hungry during the night, choose a small snack rather than a meal. | [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663163/) |
| MOVE-01 | strong | Kuipers et al. 2007: blood clots after flights of 4 hours or more | The risk of a blood clot is higher in the weeks after flights of 4 hours or more, though it stays low overall. | Moving your legs and staying hydrated are simple ways to look after yourself on long sectors. | Drink water regularly and move around or flex your calves when duties allow. | [source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17896862) |
| NAP-01 | moderate | NASA Technical Memorandum 108839 (Rosekind et al., 1994) | Pilots given a 40-minute rest opportunity on long flights slept about 26 minutes on average and had fewer attention lapses and micro-sleeps afterwards. | This study was on pilots, not cabin crew. Other research finds that naps of about 10–20 minutes help quickly, while naps of 30 minutes or more can leave you groggy for 15–30 minutes. | Aim for a 10–20 minute nap and allow time to wake up properly before safety tasks or driving. | [source](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf) |
| NAP-02 | moderate | Dinges et al. 1987; Hilditch & McHill 2019: naps before night duty and sleep inertia | A nap taken before a night of duty helps alertness more than one taken later in the night. | Longer naps give more benefit but cause more grogginess on waking (sleep inertia), usually 15–30 minutes and sometimes longer. | Finish the nap well before you leave for the airport so any grogginess has passed. | [source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/) |
| PREP-01 | practical | Practical tip, not from a specific study | This time is worked back from your report time using your own travel and getting-ready settings. | In an FAA survey of US flight attendants, fear of oversleeping was one of the main things disturbing sleep before duty. A reliable alarm helps. | Set more than one alarm, and pack your bag, documents and uniform the evening before. | — |
| PREP-02 | practical | Practical tip, not from a specific study | Briefing and safety checks need your full attention. | Arriving rested, fed and with time to spare makes the start of duty calmer. | Have water and a light snack before you report if you have not eaten recently. | — |
| PREP-03 | practical | Practical tip, not from a specific study | The time between sign-off and sleep is often longer than expected. | In an FAA survey of US flight attendants, getting from the aircraft door to the hotel took 49 minutes on average. | If you are heading to sleep in daylight, keep sunglasses handy for the trip. | [source](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf) |
| SLEEP-01 | strong | Mollicone et al. 2008: total sleep per 24 hours and performance | Alertness depends mainly on total sleep in each 24 hours, whether it comes in one block or a main sleep plus a nap. | Most adults need about 7 hours or more. Regularly getting 6 hours or less builds up effects that people tend not to notice in themselves. | Keep the room dark and cool (about 18–20 °C). An eye mask and earplugs help in hotels. | [source](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19194521) |
| SLEEP-02 | moderate | Fatigue Management Guide for Airline Operators (2015) | Recovering from short or disrupted sleep usually takes more than one night; the guide suggests two consecutive nights of unrestricted sleep. | After a night or early duty, sleeping soon after you get home or to the hotel, in a dark room, makes the most of the time available. | Wind down with dim light for a short while before bed, and keep the room dark and cool. | [source](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf) |
