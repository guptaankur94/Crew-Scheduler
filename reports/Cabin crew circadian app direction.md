# Trade pin-perfect timing for trustworthy crew guidance

CrewCircadian should become a calm, ad-free, roster-aware daily planner that turns a roster the crew member has checked into one clear next action at a time. To get there, it has to drop the promise of "pin-perfect" accuracy. The science supports exact times only for facts the roster fixes: when to leave, how long the real sleep opportunity is, when to stop caffeine before a planned sleep, and when the alarm must ring. Anything that depends on where a person's body clock actually is (the best time for light, melatonin or a nap) can only honestly be shown as a window of roughly ±1 to ±4 hours. The best validated fatigue models predict **group averages, not individuals** ([Roma et al. 2012, FAA](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf)). Even with wearable data, the body-clock estimate for shift workers misses by **2.5–3.8 hours on average** ([Huang et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/10/zsab126/6278480)). The current app falls well short of these standards. It is a fixed day-by-day template that shows minute-level times and a "SAFTE" 0–100 score that can take only nine values. It has settings that change nothing and "DGCA/FAA compliant" badges with no logic behind them. It carries unsourced statistics, and its salary estimate quietly adds 16 standby hours and 2 layovers the roster never mentioned. On its own default roster, it even tells a crew member to sleep through a flight. A nine-member advisory panel agreed unanimously on ad-free, calm, private and roster-aware. It disagreed on salary, workouts and wearables, and it rejected the "pin-perfect" claim. In the decision matrix, an honest rule-based planner scores **4.2 out of 5** and the current approach scores **1.5**. The recommended first release (MVP) contains roster import with a confirmation step, sleep-opportunity planning with reliable alarms, naps, caffeine, simple light-and-dark rules, and a plain "why" behind every suggestion. The 0–100 score, the salary studio, the compliance badges and melatonin dosing should all go. The way to earn the owner's goal of high accuracy is to measure the app with real crew, not to claim accuracy.

A note on terms: technical words are explained in plain English the first time they appear, and a full glossary sits near the end. When this report says "the owner", it means you.

## No published model can deliver minute-perfect body-clock timing from a roster

The owner's central ambition is recommendations that are "pin perfect and highly accurate". The panel's most important pushback is that this ambition, taken literally, cannot be met by anyone today. That includes Timeshifter, NASA-funded tools and airline fatigue software. Promising it would make CrewCircadian less trustworthy, not more.

Every established fatigue model is a **biomathematical model**, meaning a set of equations that estimates alertness from sleep and work times. SAFTE-FAST, FAID, the Three-Process Model and their cousins are all built on the same two ingredients: sleep pressure, which builds the longer you are awake, and a roughly 24-hour body-clock rhythm. In a blind comparison of six such models, none predicted chronic sleep restriction well. For one of them, the predictive power was "almost the same as that of a horizontal line" ([Van Dongen 2004](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Van_Dongen_ASEM_75_3_2004.pdf)). The study closest to CrewCircadian's users followed 178 US flight attendants. It found strong agreement (R² = 0.88), but only after performance was **averaged across groups of similar predictions**. The FAA authors state plainly that "most model predictions represent a population average", and that predicting individuals in "exceptionally mobile populations such as commercial aviation" remains unsolved ([Roma et al. 2012](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf)). A 136-aircrew study found an individual error of about **1.4 points on a 9-point sleepiness scale** ([Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)). That is too wide to tell "a bit tired" from "very sleepy" for one person at one hour. Regulators reach the same conclusion. Australia's CASA says models predict "a target population average rather than instantaneous fatigue levels of a specific individual" ([CASA](https://www.casa.gov.au/biomathematical-fatigue-models-guidance)). IATA's 2025 guidance says model outputs "may not be accurate for specific individuals" and must never be the sole go/no-go tool ([IATA HFTF 2025](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/iata_hftf_bmm_limitations_of_fatigue_model_guidance_april_2025.pdf)).

Timing light advice is even harder, because it depends on **circadian phase**: where your internal clock is pointing right now. Scientists usually mark this with **DLMO**, the time in the evening when melatonin starts to rise, or with **CBTmin**, the lowest point of body temperature, usually a few hours before you wake. For people on regular day schedules wearing a watch, models estimate DLMO within about 0.6–1.0 hours. For shift workers, error jumps to **2.5–2.8 hours with activity data and 3.6–3.8 hours with light data**. Using sleep times as a proxy, the error is 3.9–6.5 hours ([Huang et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/10/zsab126/6278480)). A chronotype questionnaire (the "lark or owl" quiz the current app offers) leaves **about a 4-hour range** of true DLMO at any given score. Its authors warn that timing light or melatonin from it "could result in the mistiming of these treatments… potentially worsening circadian misalignment" ([Kantermann et al. 2015](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58750/1/Kantermann_Comparing_the_morningness-eveningness_questionnaire.pdf)). People also differ more than 50-fold in how strongly evening light affects them ([Phillips et al. 2019](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1901824116)). A roster-only app for cabin crew is in the least favourable position of all: no light sensor, no activity data and a rotating schedule. A realistic honest uncertainty is **±2–4 hours** on rotating rosters, narrowing to roughly ±1–1.5 hours during a stable run of daytime duties.

The way through is to split recommendations into two kinds. **Roster facts** can be stated exactly, because they are arithmetic on the roster rather than guesses about biology. For example: "Leave home 03:15. Sleep opportunity 19:40–02:30. Last coffee before 13:30 if you plan to sleep at 19:40." **Biological advice** should be a window whose width honestly reflects what the app knows, such as "best nap window roughly 14:00–17:00" or "keep bright light low until about 08:00 Delhi time". Strong light prescriptions should be avoided when uncertainty is high, because light at the wrong side of CBTmin shifts the clock in the wrong direction ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). Honesty costs little. Experiments show that stating uncertainty as a numeric range barely affects trust, while vague hedging such as "results may vary" slightly reduces it ([van der Bles et al. 2020](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-communicating-uncertainty-on-public-trust-in-facts/)). So "accurate" should mean "never wrong about the roster, and honest about the body". That is a standard CrewCircadian can actually meet and then prove.

## The existing app promises far more than its code delivers

A line-by-line reading of the current code (branch `hardening-and-local-setup`) found a well-intentioned prototype with a credibility problem. Every recommendation comes from one function in `src/services/circadianCalculator.ts`. It sorts each calendar day (in UTC, the universal reference time used in aviation) into one of four duty types by the first report time, then fills in fixed clock times. Examples are bed at 23:00, caffeine cutoff at 17:00, an eating window from 08:30 to 18:30, and a nap at 13:00. It never looks at the neighbouring day, the crew member's actual location, or the rest between duties. On the default sample roster this produces instructions that contradict each other. A "pre-duty anchor sleep" overlaps the previous night's flight. A 03:45 wake alarm and a "crew car arrives at your door" fire while the crew member is in Dubai. "Wear dark sunglasses" and "go outside without sunglasses" are scheduled for the same hour. A "pre-flight nap" sits inside the core night's sleep. New users see these errors first, because the sample roster loads by default (`src/App.tsx:64-68`).

The trust problems are larger than the timing bugs. The "SAFTE {n}/100" score borrows the name of a validated US Army model but is a simple time-of-day lookup. Without wearable input it can show only **nine possible values** (`circadianCalculator.ts:1230-1351`). FDP compliance (whether a duty breaks legal duty-time limits) is hard-coded to `true`. Yet the interface shows "DGCA & FAA Limits Verified" and "FAR Part 117 / DGCA FDP Compliant" (`TimelineView.tsx:198, 304`), and FAR Part 117 covers pilots, not cabin crew. Several settings do nothing at all: chronotype, the melatonin "Phase Response Curve" timer, the bunk-shift selector and preferred nap length. The "Lark" card nonetheless promises "Sleep shifted 45m earlier" (`SettingsView.tsx:155`). Fourteen citation objects name organisations but give no year, document or link. Several carry statistics no study supports, such as "cortisol spikes by 32%". The NASA nap figures are stated with the two metrics swapped in different places. The salary studio adds an assumed 16 standby hours and 2 layovers to every month. Loading a sample marks a payslip as "Verified". The interface has 7 main screens, about 6 pop-up windows, and live clocks ticking in seconds. Military-style jargon (WOCL, HBT, Tactical, AIMS) appears dozens of times. Meanwhile the light and sunglasses advice that matters most for safety is computed but never shown on the Today screen. No automated tests cover the calculator, and the live Gemini roster-reading path has never been run with a real key.

None of this means starting from zero. The technology stack is sound: React and TypeScript (the languages the app is written in), wrapped by Capacitor (a tool that packages a web app as an iPhone and Android app). The idea of subtracting grooming and commute time from report time is exactly right. So are the calendar export and the instinct to use AI to read messy rosters. The table below sorts the existing app into what to keep, change, remove and build.

## Nine directors, one direction, several real dissents

The owner asked for a board that would not simply agree. Each director below gives a verdict and says plainly where they agree and disagree with the owner's vision.

### Chronobiology and sleep medicine: build on sleep opportunity, treat light with respect

The chronobiologist's verdict is that the app's backbone should be **protecting sleep opportunity and keeping the body clock stable**, not chasing exact clock times. The highest-value rules have solid evidence. Adults need roughly 7 or more hours of sleep per 24 hours, and it can be split into a main sleep plus a nap. Performance depends mainly on total sleep per day, however it is divided ([Mollicone et al. 2008](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19194521)). Six hours or less, night after night, builds deficits that people do not notice in themselves ([Van Dongen et al. 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12683469)). On short layovers, the best evidence favours keeping home-base timing. Flight attendants on a 50-hour, 9-time-zone layover had far less jet lag when they kept home sleep hours ([Lowden & Åkerstedt 1998](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9706413)). Light is the strongest lever of all: a long bright pulse can shift the clock by up to about 5 hours ([Khalsa et al. 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12717008)). But that same power is why light timing does the most harm when the clock estimate is wrong. **Agrees** with the owner that timing matters across sleep, naps and light, and that crew deserve a tool built for them. **Disagrees** with "pin-perfect" light and melatonin times. Disagrees with the chronotype setting's claim of a 45-minute shift. Disagrees with a red-eye "melatonin shield" scheduled with no check of sunrise or location. The director wants melatonin limited to information only, never a dosing timer.

### Aviation fatigue and regulation: a personal planner inside the airline's system, never a verdict

The aviation-fatigue director's verdict is to position CrewCircadian as a **personal countermeasure planner that sits inside, not against, the airline's fatigue system**. The airline's system is called an FRMS (Fatigue Risk Management System): the formal process for managing fatigue and handling fatigue reports. The most useful official source for personal advice is the ICAO/IATA/IFALPA Fatigue Management Guide. It covers caffeine, naps, recovery sleep and light. It also admits its examples "focus on flight crew" because cabin-crew research is thin ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). That honesty should carry into the app. The real cabin-crew problems are well documented. In a 9,180-person FAA survey, **84% reported fatigue and 52% had "nodded off" on a flight**. Early reports, quick turns, short layovers and missed meals were the top drivers. "Fear of oversleeping" was the second-biggest hotel sleep disruptor. Door-to-hotel time averaged 49 minutes ([CAMI 2009](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)). The legal "rest period" is therefore not the same as the sleep opportunity. **Agrees** that Indian and Gulf crew are a timely market. DGCA is tightening cabin-crew rules: draft Issue V, issued October 2025 and still unfinalised in 2026, raises weekly rest to 48 hours ([Wingmanlog](https://www.wingmanlog.in/post/understanding-cabin-crew-fdtl-guidelines-dgca-car-7-j-i); [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/air-india-indigo-lobby-dgca-to-ease-draft-cabin-crew-fatigue-regulations)). **Disagrees** with any compliance badge or legal-limit checker in the first release. The rules differ between DGCA's in-force Issue IV, the draft Issue V and the UAE's GCAA rules. Even the WOCL (window of circadian low, the regulatory "02:00–06:00" body-clock night) is defined differently by each regulator and moves with acclimatisation. Each airline sets its own report times in non-public manuals. A wrong "compliant" label is worse than none.

### Physiology and exercise science: workouts help a little, so do not make them a headline

The exercise physiologist's verdict is that timed exercise is **a modest helper, not a pillar on par with sleep and light**. Morning exercise advanced the clock by about 0.6 hours after five days ([Thomas et al. 2020](https://insight.jci.org/articles/view/134270)). The best and worst times follow a curve similar to light's, but much weaker ([Youngstedt et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6462487/)). The firmer, safety-relevant rules are about what *not* to do. Hard sessions that end within about 4 hours of sleep were linked to later and shorter sleep across 4 million nights ([Leota et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/)). Sleep loss cuts physical performance by about 7.6% ([Craven et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35708888)). Strength and power peak in the late afternoon and evening ([Knaier et al. 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34431827)). **Agrees** that crew value fitness. Air India now ties cabin-crew rostering to BMI ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/travel/airline-cracks-down-crews-weight-fitness-health-warning-some-could-pulled-from-flights)), so movement advice has real pull. **Disagrees** with giving workouts equal billing in version one. The director recommends two one-line guardrails for the MVP: "finish hard sessions at least 4 hours before planned sleep" and "after a night flight, recovery sleep first, gentle movement only". Full workout windows wait for Phase 2. The app should never discourage gentle movement after long flights, and should list warning signs of blood clots. Clot risk roughly triples in the weeks after flights of 4 hours or more, though it remains low in absolute terms ([Kuipers et al. 2007](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17896862)).

### Nutrition and caffeine: one robust meal rule, and a caffeine cutoff that depends on the dose

The nutrition director's verdict is to replace the current "Salk 10h Protocol" eating windows with **one well-supported rule**: eat the main meal before a night duty, and if hungry in the body's night, have a small snack rather than a meal. In a 52-person randomised trial, a meal at 00:30 worsened next-day glucose tolerance much more than a snack did ([Centofanti et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663163/)). In the same programme, the snack group was the most alert at 04:00 ([Gupta et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2019.1676256)). The Salk Institute's dramatic results come mostly from mice. Large human trials find time-restricted eating no better than ordinary calorie restriction ([Liu et al. 2022, NEJM](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833); [Semnani-Azad et al. 2025, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082007)). The British Dietetic Association says evidence on shift-work meal timing "is not yet strong enough to make firm recommendations" ([BDA](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/nutrition-shift-work.html)). Caffeine is different: trials give real numbers. About 100 mg (one coffee) up to 4 hours before bed had no measurable effect on sleep. 400 mg should stop at least 12 hours before ([Gardiner et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985402/)). Caffeine hurts daytime recovery sleep more than night sleep ([Carrier et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.npp.1301198)). Safe limits are 400 mg a day, 200 mg per dose, and 200 mg a day in pregnancy ([EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine)). **Agrees** that eating and caffeine timing belong in the app. **Disagrees** with minute-precise eating windows. Disagrees with the red-eye "gut rest" fast ("light almonds only"), because fasting through a safety-critical duty gave no performance benefit over a snack. For Indian users, the director adds that chai should count toward the caffeine budget. It should also sit an hour away from meals to protect iron absorption ([ICMR-NIN 2024](https://newsonair.gov.in/icmr-releases-updated-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-in-2024)). Ramadan and pregnancy need their own gentle modes.

### Psychology and behaviour change: one next action, no scores, and a plan the user can bend

The psychologist's verdict is that the owner's "no mental effort" instinct is right. The app should show **one next action, tied to roster events rather than clock habits**. Rotating rosters break the stable daily cues that habits need: forming a habit takes a median of about 66 days ([Lally, via University of Surrey](https://www.surrey.ac.uk/news/does-it-really-take-66-days-form-habit-we-asked-expert-dr-pippa-lally)). "If–then" plans work well instead, such as "when you reach the hotel, then darken the room and sleep until 14:30". They have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (effect size d = 0.65 across 94 studies, [Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/handle/123456789/10973)). Shift-work apps lose users fast. Sleepfit users touched only 17% of its content ([Sleepfit 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15402002.2025.2476687)), and the median mental-health app keeps 3.3% of users at day 30 ([Baumel et al. 2019](https://www.jmir.org/2019/9/e14567/)). Value must arrive in a glance, without daily logging. **Agrees** with calm, minimal and ad-free. **Disagrees** with the 0–100 score. Being told you slept badly makes people feel worse the next day, even when their measured vigilance is unchanged ([Gavriloff et al. 2018](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jsr.12726)). About 31% of sleep trackers screen positive for "orthosomnia" risk, an unhealthy fixation on perfect sleep data ([SLEEP 2026 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/49/Supplement_1/A246/8674102)). The director also cautions that "zero mental math" must not mean "zero checking". People keep using an imperfect algorithm when they can adjust it ([Dietvorst et al. 2018](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/10.1287/mnsc.2016.2643)). A 30-second roster confirmation and a "move this nap" button therefore build trust rather than add burden.

### Product and UX design: three screens, not seven

The designer's verdict is to **collapse seven screens and six pop-ups into three core screens plus settings**, built on Calm Technology principles. These ask a product to "require the smallest possible amount of attention", "work even when it fails" and use "the minimum technology needed" ([Calm Technology](https://calmtech.com/)). Today, the same sleep, meal and caffeine blocks appear on two near-identical screens. Grooming and commute controls appear in three places. A two-sector early day shows roughly 30 badges and 30 clock times. **Agrees** completely with the calm, minimal, no-redundancy vision; the current app is simply far from it. **Disagrees** with the implied wish to show all six pillars every day. A calm product shows only what matters in the next few hours. On a quiet day off, that may be a single line: "Free day. Bed around 23:00 keeps your clock steady for Thursday's 05:30 report."

### Technology: a predictable engine on the phone, AI only for reading

The technology director's verdict is that all advice should come from a **deterministic engine running on the phone**: plain rules whose output is the same every time for the same input, and which can be tested. Gemini's role should be limited to reading rosters. Gemini's own documentation says structured output guarantees correctly shaped data, not correct values: "always validate values in your application" ([Gemini docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/structured-output)). Roster import should therefore try the most reliable route first. Calendar or spreadsheet files come first, then text extracted from PDFs, then on-phone text recognition (OCR, software that reads text from images). Gemini comes last, as a consented fallback. Every import should end with automatic checks: airports exist, each arrival is after its departure, each leg's arrival matches the next departure, and duties do not overlap. A human confirmation screen comes after the checks. Under the hood, events should be stored as UTC plus the airport's named time-zone ID, using an MIT-licensed airport-to-time-zone table ([airportsdata](https://github.com/mborsetti/airportsdata)). Data should live in an on-device SQLite database rather than web storage. Wake alarms need native support. Apple's AlarmKit (iOS 26+) breaks through Silent mode ([Apple AlarmKit](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/alarmkit)). Android 14 no longer grants exact alarms by default ([Android](https://developer.android.com/about/versions/14/changes/schedule-exact-alarms)). iOS keeps at most 64 pending notifications per app ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/811171)), so reminders must be scheduled on a rolling basis. **Agrees** that Capacitor and AI-assisted reading are sensible choices. **Disagrees** that AI can make advice accurate; only tested rules and verified rosters can. The director wants a light-driven body-clock model in Phase 2. Arcascope's open-source `circadian` package is MIT-licensed and cheap enough to run on a phone ([GitHub](https://github.com/Arcascope/circadian)), but it needs a careful port and tests. It is not a launch feature.

### Legal, privacy and regulation: wellness wording, paid AI tier, data on the phone

The legal director's verdict is to keep CrewCircadian clearly in **"general wellness" territory**. The US FDA's January 2026 guidance lists "sleep management" as wellness. It excludes products that name specific diseases, give "medication dosing", or claim "clinical grade" ([FDA 2026](https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download)). Practically, this means the app should not mention "shift work disorder" or "insomnia treatment", should give no melatonin doses, and should never say "fit for duty" or "medical-grade". Apple bans drug-dose calculators from unapproved developers ([App Store Review Guidelines 1.4.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Gemini's free tier lets Google use submitted content and warns "do not submit… personal information". Its terms also forbid using it "to provide medical advice" ([Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms)). Apple now requires explicit consent before sending personal data to third-party AI (guideline 5.1.2(i)). India's DPDP privacy rules bring most obligations into force around **13 May 2027**: a plain-language notice, easy withdrawal of consent and 72-hour breach reporting ([DPDP Rules 2025](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/53450e6e5dc0bfa85ebd78686cadad39.pdf)). Crew also have a personal legal duty not to operate if they "know or suspect" they are fatigued ([GCAA CAT.GEN.MPA.100](https://www.gcaa.gov.ae/en/epublication/EPublications/Civil%20Aviation%20Regulations%20(CARs)/CAR%20IV%20-%20FLIGHT%20OPERATIONS%20REGULATIONS/CAR%20AIR%20OPS/CAR-AIR%20OPS%20-%20PART-CAT%20-%20ISSUE%2001.pdf)). A reassuring "85/100" shown to someone who feels unfit is the app's largest liability exposure. **Agrees** that ad-free and private-by-design build trust and reduce legal risk. **Disagrees** with the payslip upload, the "DGCA/FAA compliant" labels, "NASA/ICAO-based" authority claims, and airline logos on sample payslips. NASA forbids implying endorsement ([Finnegan](https://www.finnegan.com/en/insights/articles/the-nasa-brand-flies-high-fulfilling-agencys-mission.html)). Airline names may appear only descriptively, for example "works with rosters from…".

### Business and competition: the gap is real, but so are the circling competitors

The business director's verdict is that the market gap is genuine. No consumer app found is built for cabin crew. Timeshifter, the leader with about 1.6 million users, says outright that it is "not intended for pilots and flight crew on duty" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/timeshifter/id1380684374)). Roster apps such as RosterBuster (owned by CAE, about 400,000 users, ad-supported, frequent complaints about bugs) give no sleep guidance ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/rosterbuster-crew-app/id1035558169)). The threats are concrete. Clairoe (formerly Arcashift) already times light, meals, caffeine, workouts and sleep, though not for rosters ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/arcashift-sleep-balance/id1589005077)). Timeshifter has fresh funding to expand into shift work ([Timeshifter](https://www.timeshifter.com/news/timeshifter-raises-new-funding-to-scale-circadian-technology-across-travel-shift-work-and-healthcare)). The Indian app AirRoster is free and ad-free and could add wellness features ([Wingman](https://www.wingmanlog.in/product-airroster-airline-roster-app-pilots-cabin-crew/)). The defensible advantages are crew-specific logic, reliability, privacy, and Indian and Gulf context. **Agrees** with ad-free and with starting in India and the Gulf. **Disagrees with fellow directors** on salary. No competitor offers Indian or Gulf crew pay estimates, which makes salary a potential way to attract users. The business director would park it as a separate, optional module rather than delete it, provided the rates are sourced and no payslip goes to AI. The director also warns that "ad-free" still needs a way to earn money. Comparable apps charge $25–70 a year, while Indian crew earn roughly ₹40,000–85,000 a month ([Wingman](https://www.wingmanlog.in/post/indigo-cabin-crew-roster-explained/)). A low annual price near AirRoster's $29 or below, with easy cancellation and no streaks, is the likely starting point. This is untested.

## The decision matrix favours an honest, roster-aware planner

The board scored six strategic options and eighteen candidate features from 1 (poor) to 5 (excellent) on seven criteria. The criteria and their weights were: evidence strength 15%, user value 20%, trust 15%, accuracy achievable 15%, regulatory risk 10% (5 = lowest risk), build effort 10% (5 = least effort), and fit with the calm/minimal vision 15%. These scores are the panel's structured judgement based on the evidence above; they are not measurements. Their job is to make the trade-offs visible and arguable.

## What the first release should contain, and what waits

**MVP** stands for "minimum viable product": the smallest version that delivers real value to real users and lets you learn from them. For CrewCircadian, the MVP promise is: *"Upload your roster, check it once, and always know your next sleep, nap, caffeine and light move, with a reason you can read."*

### The MVP rule book

The engine should start from a short, auditable set of rules. Each rule is tied to a source and an evidence grade (strong, moderate or weak), and each should be reviewed by a sleep physician before launch.

| Situation | What the app says (plain words) | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every duty | Exact "leave by", "wake by" and "sleep opportunity" times. The opportunity is net of grooming, commute, post-flight time (DGCA: 30 min, 60 min international with medical) and hotel transfer | Strong (arithmetic) | [DGCA CAR 7-J-I Issue IV](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf); [CAMI 2009](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf) |

## Design and tone: one next action, plain words, no scores

The design principles follow directly from the evidence above. **Show one next action**, with everything else one tap away. **Be exact about roster facts and honest about biology**: "Alarm 02:45" is exact, while "keep light low until roughly 07:00 Delhi time" is a window, and both say so. **Anchor advice to roster events**, not clock habits, because "when you reach the hotel, darken the room" survives a changing roster where "sleep at 14:00 daily" does not. **No numbers about the person**: no score, no sleep debt to one decimal place, no streaks, no red "fatigue risk" banners. Any harder-day note uses words and reasons, such as "Long day with an early start; protect this afternoon's nap". Users must be able to **bend the plan** ("I can't sleep then", "skip this nap") and have it re-plan. Experts on shift-worker guidelines asked for exactly this "softening of the language", plus acceptance that "everyone is different" ([Shriane et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10710992/)).

Tone should sound like a calm senior colleague: warm, brief and never judgmental. Replace pilot and military jargon with plain words. "Cockpit", "Tactical", "WOCL", "HBT" and "SAFTE" become "your body-clock night" and "home time". Use simple international English, because Gulf carriers' crew come from more than 140 nationalities ([Emirates](https://www.emirates.com/media-centre/celebrating-international-cabin-crew-day-the-emirates-way/)). Keep Indian touches where they help, such as counting chai as caffeine and offering vegetarian snack examples. Avoid weight or body-image framing, especially given airline BMI policies.

Notifications should be **opt-in, few and tied to roster decision points**: before report, on landing, on reaching the hotel, and before planned sleep. They must never fire during a planned sleep. Batching notifications into a few per day measurably improved people's mood and sense of control ([Fitz et al. 2019](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Batching-smartphone-notifications-can-improve-Fitz-Kushlev/ce19cf66bdc6ced56a2f16d2483d97f9dc59b21d)). Safety shapes content too. Light advice on layovers should suggest a hotel window or lobby, never a walk outside at night in an unfamiliar city. An Air India crew member was assaulted in her London layover hotel in 2024 ([AeroTime](https://www.aerotime.aero/articles/air-india-cabin-crew-assaulted-in-london-heathrow-hotel)). Hotel names should not be stored. One sentence should be permanently visible: "If you feel fatigued, follow your airline's fatigue procedure, whatever this app shows." The wake alarm should also carry a reminder that it must not be the only alarm before a report.

Privacy is part of the design, not a policy page. All planning happens on the phone and works in flight mode. Nothing goes to an airline. Cloud roster reading is an explicit, named choice ("Ask Google Gemini to read this image"). Export and delete are one tap each. Nearly three-quarters (72%) of Europeans surveyed fear health-data misuse, and only 9% would share with private companies ([Causio et al. 2025](https://academic.oup.com/eurpub/article/35/3/401/8116209)), so visible restraint is a selling point.

## Legal and privacy guardrails to put in place before launch

Before launch, publish through a company rather than as an individual developer. Apple expects health and "air travel" apps from a legal entity, and Google Play wants organisation accounts with a D-U-N-S number (a business identification number) for health apps ([Apple 5.1.1(ix)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/); [Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/13634885?hl=en)). Carry four disclaimers, modelled on Timeshifter's store wording ([Google Play listing](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.timeshifter.timeshifter&hl=en_US)):

1. A wellness disclaimer: "not a medical device and does not diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any medical condition", as Google Play requires ([Play policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16679511?hl=en)).
2. An aviation disclaimer: "not a fatigue risk management tool; does not assess fitness for duty".
3. A research disclaimer: NASA, ICAO and others have not endorsed the app.
4. An airline disclaimer: the app is independent of IndiGo, Air India, Emirates and Etihad.

If Gemini stays, use only a billing-enabled (paid) project. Keep the key on a server, set the shortest log retention, send cropped images without names or staff numbers, and show Apple's AI consent screen first ([Gemini logs policy](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/logs-policy)). Set an 18+ audience, which Gemini's terms also require. In Google Play's health declaration, choose only wellness categories, such as Sleep Management and Activity and Fitness. Declaring a medication or decision-support category invites medical-app scrutiny. Indian consumer law may void US-style liability waivers, so clear warnings during onboarding matter more than fine print ([Nishith Desai](https://www.nishithdesai.com/fileadmin/user_upload/pdfs/NDA%20Hotline/FAQ-Consumer-Protection-Act-A4.pdf)). Have Indian counsel review the terms.

## Glossary in plain English

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circadian rhythm / body clock | The roughly 24-hour internal clock that times sleepiness, alertness, hormones and digestion |
| Circadian phase | Where your body clock is pointing right now, which may differ from the clock on the wall after travel or night work |
| DLMO | The time in the evening when melatonin (the "darkness hormone") begins to rise; scientists use it to mark body-clock time |
| CBTmin | The lowest point of body temperature, usually a few hours before you wake. Light after it shifts the clock earlier; light before it shifts the clock later |
| Phase response curve (PRC) | A chart showing how much light, melatonin or exercise shifts the clock depending on when it happens |
| Sleep inertia | Grogginess after waking, worst after long naps or naps at night |
| WOCL | "Window of circadian low": the regulators' fixed 02:00–06:00 (or 05:59) box when most people are least alert |
| FDP / FDTL | Flight duty period / flight duty time limitations: the legal maximum working hours and minimum rest |
| FRMS | Fatigue Risk Management System: an airline's formal process for managing fatigue and handling fatigue reports |
| Biomathematical model | Equations that estimate alertness from sleep and work times (e.g., SAFTE-FAST); accurate for groups, not individuals |
| MVP | Minimum viable product: the smallest version that delivers real value and lets you learn |
| Deterministic engine | Code that always gives the same answer for the same input, so it can be tested and explained |
| OCR | Optical character recognition: software that reads text from images |
| LLM / Gemini | A large language model, an AI that reads and writes text; Gemini is Google's |
| API / paid tier | The connection your app uses to send data to Gemini; the paid tier stops Google using your data to improve its products |
| Capacitor | A tool that wraps a web app so it runs as an iPhone and Android app |
| SQLite | A small database stored on the phone that, unlike web storage, the system will not quietly delete |
| UTC / IANA time zone | UTC is the universal reference time; an IANA zone (e.g., "Asia/Kolkata") is a named rule set that includes daylight-saving changes |
| HealthKit / Health Connect | Apple's and Android's stores of health data (sleep, steps) that apps can read with permission |
| Orthosomnia | Anxiety caused by chasing "perfect" sleep numbers from an app or tracker |

## Conclusion

The owner's instinct for calm, ad-free, roster-aware help is well supported, and the market gap is real. The weakness is in the wording of the ambition. "Pin-perfect" is a promise about biology that no model in the field can keep, and the current app tried to look precise in place of being right. The more durable advantage is to be the one crew app that is never wrong about the roster and always honest about the body. That means exact alarms and sleep opportunities, honest windows, and a reason behind every suggestion. That standard can be tested, published and improved. A claimed 0–100 score cannot.

This also changes what "accuracy" means for the roadmap. It becomes something measured with crew, not something declared on a screen: how often the roster is read correctly, how often suggested sleep is actually taken, and whether windows narrow as feedback and the light-driven model arrive in Phase 2. If CrewCircadian gathers that evidence from Indian and Gulf crew, it will hold something no competitor yet has: real-world data on how cabin crew sleep around their rosters. That data, more than any feature, is the asset most likely to sustain both trust and the business.
