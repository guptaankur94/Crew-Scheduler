# Skip the long nap, take the early night

After an early-start duty (report 05:00, home about 17:00) with a standby from 12:00 the next day, the evidence favours an **earlier, longer main sleep** over a recovery nap. If the crew member needs a nap to stay safely awake until bedtime, it should be **short (about 10–20 minutes, with an alarm) and finished by about 18:00**. A **"1–2 hour max" nap is the least supported option of all**. At 17:00–19:00, after a 03:30 wake-up, a 1–2 hour nap falls in the range where grogginess is worst, and it uses up the sleep pressure that would otherwise make an early night easy. No study supports any fixed nap cap. The review that tested the popular "30 minutes or less" rule called universal rules "potentially misleading" without the time of day and the person's recent sleep ([Hilditch, Dorrian & Banks 2017](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/3314e5ee-e2af-4e68-a541-99e22d7a654d)). The strongest evidence in this report is that more recovery sleep helps in proportion to its length, and that one long night helps but does not fully reset someone who is short of sleep ([Banks et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910531)). The free evening should still be a real evening. A proper early meal, a walk in daylight, time with family or friends, dimmer lights after about 19:00 and no alcohol all fit with a bedtime 1–2 hours earlier than usual. A hard workout fits better the next morning. The app currently plans a **60-minute "nap after an early duty"** and cites a NASA in-flight nap study for it. The evidence supports changing this: make the default a short nap or an earlier night, never a 1-hour block. One caveat runs through everything below. **No study has tested what crew should do on the evening after an early duty.** The plan is built from lab studies of healthy volunteers and from pilot field data, so a sleep doctor should review it before it ships.

## Early starts leave crew about 1.5–2.5 hours short before they get home

The problem starts the night before. Among 70 short-haul pilots, sleep in the 12 hours before duty was lowest for reports between 04:00 and 05:00, at **5.4 hours**. Pilots lost about **15 minutes of sleep for every hour the report moved earlier than 09:00** ([Roach et al. 2012](https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Duty_periods_with_early_start_times_restrict_the_amount_of_sleep_obtained_by_short-haul_airline_pilots/13464182/1)). In a later study of 94 short-haul pilots, the average wake-up before an early start was **04:19**. Their average bedtime was 21:15, and they reported sleeping less than their own sleep need of 7.76 hours ([Arsintescu et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9286802)). The official fatigue guide gives the reason going to bed earlier does not fix this. The few hours before a person's usual bedtime are a **"wake maintenance zone"**, when the body clock pushes hard against sleep. That makes it "very difficult to fall asleep earlier than usual, ahead of an early duty report time" ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)).

So a cabin crew member reporting at 05:00 probably woke around 03:30 after about 5 hours of sleep. By 17:00 they have been awake about 13.5 hours and are roughly 1.5–2.5 hours short of their usual sleep. These figures come from pilots. **No study measures cabin crew sleep before early starts, or naps after them.** The mechanism, which is sleep being squeezed by the report time, does not depend on the job, so the pilot numbers are a fair estimate for cabin crew. The fatigue guide itself admits that cabin crew fatigue "has received much more scientific… attention" for flight crew than for cabin crew ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). Two points from that guide shape everything that follows. First, "some sleep is always better than none". Second, lost sleep "is not recovered hour-for-hour", and **two full nights in a row** are needed to recover from a sleep debt.

## A short nap or an early night beats the 1–2 hour middle

### Nap length decides how groggy you wake up

The best controlled data on nap length come from healthy adults who were allowed only about 5 hours in bed and then napped at about 15:00. A **10-minute nap** improved sleepiness, mood and performance straight away, and some of the benefit lasted about 2.5 hours. A **30-minute nap** caused a spell of impaired alertness on waking (sleep inertia) before its benefits arrived. A 5-minute nap did almost nothing ([Brooks & Lack 2006, summarised](https://evanhahn.com/naps-and-their-effectiveness)). A review of nap research reached the same view. Brief naps of 5–15 minutes help almost immediately for 1–3 hours. Naps over 30 minutes bring grogginess first and a longer benefit afterwards ([Lovato & Lack 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21075238/)). In a simulated night shift, a 30-minute nap caused sleep inertia and a 10-minute nap did not. Recovery took **up to 45 minutes**, and those volunteers were well rested to begin with ([Sleep/Wake Research Centre](https://sleepwake.ac.nz/projects/sleep-inertia-after-naps)). Grogginess is "exaggerated by prior sleep loss and the time of day" at which a person wakes ([Hilditch & McHill 2019](https://doaj.org/article/29662a41abeb460dba2c815c9400e14a)).

This matters for crew because someone who woke at 03:30 has high sleep pressure. They will probably reach deep sleep sooner within any nap than the rested lab volunteers did. That would make even a 20–30 minute nap groggier for them. This is a reasoned inference, not a measured result. The popular claim that a **90-minute "full-cycle" nap avoids grogginess could not be verified** and should not be used.

### A late, long nap borrows from the night

Timing matters as much as length. In healthy young men, an early-evening nap **delayed sleep onset the following night and reduced the build-up of deep sleep** in it ([Werth et al. 1996, Surrey record](https://openresearch.surrey.ac.uk/permalink/44SUR_INST/15d8lgh/alma99515648202346)). Sleep researchers explain this with the "two-process" model: an afternoon nap releases sleep pressure, and the pressure that builds afterwards "may be insufficient" by the usual bedtime ([Sleep Society](https://www.sleepsociety.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Regulation_of_the_sleep-wake_cycle.pdf)). The steelman case for longer naps is real. Healthy older adults who took **2-hour afternoon naps ending by 18:00** for a month slept almost exactly as long at night (7:57 vs 8:06) and gained about 70–88 minutes of total sleep per day ([Campbell et al. 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3074345)). However, those naps started around 15:00, the people were 50–88 years old, and they were not short of sleep. That makes them a poor match for a 30-year-old crew member lying down at 17:30 after a 03:30 alarm.

### No fixed cap has evidence behind it

The 2017 review of short naps tested the most common industry rule, "nap 30 minutes or less", and found mixed results on when deep sleep starts and how bad the grogginess is. It concluded that without the time of day and the person's recent sleep and wake history, the rule "is potentially misleading" ([Hilditch, Dorrian & Banks 2017](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/3314e5ee-e2af-4e68-a541-99e22d7a654d)). The other familiar rules, "no naps after 15:00" and "avoid naps", come from insomnia treatment and sleep-hygiene convention, not from trials in healthy people who are short of sleep ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/how-sleep-works/does-napping-impact-sleep-at-night); [PsychDB CBT-I](https://psychdb.com/psychotherapy/cbt-insomnia)). Those rules aim to build sleep pressure in people who struggle to fall asleep. A crew member home at 17:00 after a 03:30 start has the opposite problem. **A "1–2 hour max" cap therefore has no evidence behind it.** It is also badly placed. It is too long to avoid grogginess, too long to leave the night's sleep pressure intact, and too short to count as recovery sleep for someone who is truly exhausted.

### The early night is the stronger lever

Recovery sleep works in proportion to dose. After five nights of 4 hours in bed, one recovery night helped more the longer it was, but even **10 hours in bed did not fully restore** reaction time, sleepiness or fatigue ([Banks et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2910531)). After a week of restricted sleep, three 8-hour nights still left people impaired ([Belenky et al. 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12603781/)). These studies used several short nights. After a single short night, one long night probably restores most function, but nobody has tested that directly. The split-sleep studies look at whether a nap plus a shorter night does as well as one block. They found **total sleep in 24 hours matters more than whether it comes in one piece**. Young men did about as well on split sleep as on one night block ([Jackson, Banks & Belenky 2014](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/investigation-of-the-effectiveness-of-a-split-sleep-schedule-in-s/)). Teenagers on 5 hours plus a 1.5-hour nap matched rested controls on memory, but the authors warn this "should not be considered a replacement for adequate nocturnal sleep" ([Lo et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6448285)). Those naps were mid-afternoon, not an hour or two before bed. **No study compares "bed at 21:00" with "nap at 17:30, then bed at 23:00" after an early start.**

The balance of evidence still points one way. An earlier bedtime adds sleep in one solid block. It avoids a second episode of grogginess and cannot weaken the night. The 12:00 standby helps here, because it removes any need for an early alarm, so a 9–10 hour sleep opportunity is realistic. The wake maintenance zone is the main objection, since it makes falling asleep early hard ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). That zone was mapped mostly in rested people ([Frontiers in Sleep 2023](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/frsle.2023.1304647/full)). After about 18 hours awake on a short night, sleep pressure probably overrides it for most people. That is an inference, which is why the app should say "go to bed when you feel sleepy, from about 21:00" rather than "be asleep by 21:00".

## A worked evening: home at 17:00, standby at 12:00

The plan below assumes the person woke about 03:30 after about 5 hours of sleep, has a usual bedtime of about 23:00, and is on home standby from 12:00. The free hours are real and should be used. In a 4-week study of 109 employees, exhaustion eroded people's ability to switch off from work. The effect was strongest in those **without pleasurable leisure** ([Sonnentag et al. 2014](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48957)). Shift workers also drop advice that ignores family and social life ([Murray et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10064476/)).

| Time | What to do | Why | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17:00–17:30 | Shower, change, a drink of water or a snack. **Only if you are fighting to stay awake:** a 10–20 minute nap with an alarm, finished by about 18:00. Otherwise skip it. | Short naps avoid grogginess. A late long nap delays and lightens the night. | Moderate (nap length); None (the 18:00 cut-off is a rule of thumb) |
| 17:30–19:00 | A proper, moderate dinner, not very fatty or spicy. Eat with family or friends if you like. | Dinner 1 hour before bed did not change overall sleep in a crossover trial ([Duan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8131073)). Late fatty food has been linked to poorer sleep ([Crispim 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3227713/)). Eating early is low-regret, not a must. | Weak–Moderate |
| Before dark | A gentle walk outdoors or some stretching. **No hard workout tonight.** | Moderate evening exercise does not harm sleep ([Stutz 2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0)). Maximal exercise ending 2 hours before sleep was linked to **36 minutes later sleep onset** ([Leota 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/)). Short sleep cuts afternoon and evening exercise performance by about **7.6%** ([Craven 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35708888)). | Strong (a walk is fine); Moderate (avoid hard sessions) |
| All evening | **No alcohol.** The legal floor is none within 12 hours of the standby start (after 00:00). | Even about 2 drinks cut REM sleep ([Gardiner 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030)). Low doses reduce the body's recovery in the first 3 hours of sleep ([Pietilä 2018](https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519)). Sleep loss and alcohol add up in sleepiness ([Roehrs et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14746378/)). | Strong (sleep); regulatory (12 h) |
| From about 19:00 | Dim the house lights and use warm lamps. A phone or TV at low brightness is fine. Wind down from about 20:30. | Even 30–50 lux in the evening delayed melatonin by 77–109 minutes in a lab. That evidence is stronger for room light than for screens ([Chang 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4313797/)). Experts did not agree that screens harm adult sleep ([JMIR 2024](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e48356)). | Moderate–Strong (room light); Weak–Moderate (screens) |
| From about 21:00 | Go to bed when sleepy, about 1–2 hours earlier than usual. Use a dark room. Set a safety alarm for about 08:30, but expect to wake naturally. | One long night is the strongest recovery tool available. | Strong (more sleep helps); None (the exact bedtime) |
| Morning | Get outdoor daylight soon after waking. A harder workout fits here if you want one. Have a normal breakfast, and a meal before 12:00 that isn't heavy. | Morning exercise is least affected by sleep loss ([Craven 2022](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35708888)). Daylight supports mood ([UK Biobank, PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/individuals-spending-more-time-in-daylight-have-lower-risk-of-mental-health-disorders/)). | Moderate / Weak |
| During standby | If you feel sleepy while waiting, nap. A late call-out can mean a long night. | The FMG says: "attempt sleep (prioritize sleep over other activities)" ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). | Moderate (official guidance) |

Social plans fit inside this plan rather than against it. An early dinner out, with food, little or no alcohol, and home about 1–1.5 hours before bed, costs very little sleep. A late night out costs most of the benefit of the 12:00 standby. The DGCA rules matter for the standby itself. A home standby lasts up to 12 hours ([Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)), so a call at 18:00 or later could mean flying into the early hours. Banking a long night and napping during the standby are the levers the crew member controls. Research on European pilots supports this: fatigue on duties assigned from home standby tracked **prior sleep**, not just hours awake ([EASA FTL2.0](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/141964/en)).

## What the app should default to, and what changes the advice

### What the app does today

The code in `src/services/planEngine.ts` plans a **60-minute "Nap after an early duty"** (`POST_EARLY_DUTY_NAP_MINUTES = 60`). The nap starts at the moment the person could be in bed: release time, plus travel (60 minutes by default), plus 30 minutes to wind down. It is planned only if that moment falls **before 16:00 home time**, and only if the nap ends at least 3 hours before the next main sleep. It is labelled optional, with the subtitle "Early starts usually cut sleep short; a nap helps you catch up". It cites `nasa_nap_26`, the NASA in-flight rest study. That study measured **26 minutes of sleep obtained in a 40-minute cockpit rest opportunity** ([NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)), so it does not support a 60-minute nap at home. The night after the early duty uses the usual bedtime (default 23:00) and a 7.5-hour target. It moves earlier only if the person logged a short sleep, and then by at most 1 hour (`SHORT_SLEEP_CATCH_UP_MAX_HOURS = 1`). In the exact scenario here (released about 16:30, could be in bed about 18:00), **the app offers no nap at all and plans a normal 23:00 bedtime**. It gives neither the short nap nor the early night that the evidence favours.

### Does the evidence support changing the 1-hour nap?

Yes, on four grounds. A 60-minute nap sits squarely in the range where grogginess appears, and grogginess is worse after sleep loss (**moderate**). The rule's citation does not support its number. No study supports 60 minutes as a default (**none**). And the evidence favours lengthening the night, which the app barely does (**strong** that more sleep helps). The fairest counter-argument is that someone napping at home at 14:30 with nothing to do afterwards does not much care about 20 minutes of grogginess, and a nap 8 hours before bed probably does little harm to the night ([Campbell 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3074345)). That argues for **keeping a longer nap as a choice the person makes**, not for keeping it as the default. Changing the nap's default length and wording is a change, not a removal. If any version of the nap card would disappear for some users, the owner's rule applies: list it in a table and get explicit approval first.

The proposed defaults below are product rules of thumb built on the evidence above. They are not values taken from any study.

| Situation (home time) | Proposed default | Evidence for the number |
|---|---|---|
| Could be in bed before about 15:00 | Optional nap, **20 minutes** (a 30-minute block to allow for falling asleep), with an alarm. Allow 15–30 minutes before driving. Offer a "longer nap" choice. | Moderate (length); None (cut-off) |
| Could be in bed about 15:00–18:00 | Optional nap of 10–20 minutes, **only "if you can't stay awake"**, ending by about 18:00. Tonight's bed 1–2 hours earlier than usual. | Moderate / None |
| Could be in bed after about 18:00 | No nap card. An early night. | None (cut-off) |
| Next morning free or standby at 10:00 or later | Lengthen tonight's sleep opportunity to about 9–10 hours. Morning alarm only as a safety net. | Strong (dose of recovery sleep) |
| Any of the above | One plain line: "If you're struggling to stay awake, go to bed now rather than napping and staying up." | Supported by FMG "some sleep is better than none"; exact wording untested |

### Inputs that should change the advice

The most important input is **how much the person slept last night**. The roster can estimate it from the wake time needed for the report, and a one-tap log can confirm it. Under about 4 hours, or if the person says they are struggling to stay awake, the app should drop the nap and suggest going to bed early with no cap, as early as about 19:30. If they slept close to their usual amount, a normal or slightly early bedtime is enough. The second input is the **next duty**. A late or night duty next means staying nearer the usual bedtime and planning a nap before the duty. It also means morning light becomes the wrong direction, because bright morning light pushes the body clock earlier, which suits early rosters and works against late ones. The third is **consecutive early starts**. Sleep debt builds across them, and recovery needs two full nights ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)), so the early-night rule should repeat each night rather than assume one night fixes it. The fourth is **release time and commute**. They decide whether any nap window exists, and grogginess after a nap matters if the person must drive. The fifth is **usual bedtime and chronotype**, which set where the wake maintenance zone falls. The sixth is **insomnia tendency**. People who already struggle to fall asleep are the group the "no evening naps" rule was written for, so for them the app should lean further toward no nap. The last is **home versus airport standby**. Airport standby counts in full as duty and moves the wake time earlier ([Reg. (EU) 83/2014](https://lawplayer.com/eu/act/32014R0083)). How sleepy the person says they feel should count, but cautiously: self-assessment "becomes less reliable" as sleepiness rises ([FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)). "I feel fine" should not override a logged 4-hour night.

## ChatGPT's answer is sound on caution, loose on sources

The ChatGPT answer's practical direction matches the evidence. It says not to cap recovery sleep for an exhausted person, to compare a nap with an earlier bedtime, and to keep workouts light. Two of its regulatory citations claim more than the source documents say.

## What remains unproven, for a sleep doctor to review

Every number specific to the scenario rests on inference. These are the questions to put to a sleep physician before the advice is built into the app, along with what the notes found on each.

|---|---|---|

## Conclusion

The question "how long should the recovery nap be?" turns out to be the wrong frame for this evening. At 17:00 after a 03:30 start, the real choice is between protecting a long night and spending sleep pressure on a nap. The night wins whenever the next morning is free. That makes the 12:00 standby an asset to plan around, not just a duty to remember. The app's job is less to time a nap and more to move bedtime earlier and lengthen the sleep window, which its current 7.5-hour, 23:00 default barely does. A short "if you can't stay awake" nap is the safety valve.

The more lasting lesson is about where the app's numbers come from. Its 60-minute nap carried a NASA citation that measured something else. Every replacement number proposed here is labelled as a rule of thumb. Wording such as "go to bed when sleepy, from about 21:00" is honest about what the evidence knows. The quickest route to real accuracy is a sleep doctor's review of the cut-offs, plus a light-touch log of what crew actually slept after early duties. That data does not exist anywhere yet, and CrewCircadian could be the first to collect it.
