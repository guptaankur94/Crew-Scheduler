## 1. ICAO Doc 9966 / ICAO-IATA-IFALPA Fatigue Management Guide (FMG) 2015: what they recommend for individual crew self-management

### Takeaway
The 2015 FMG (2nd edition of the 2011 FRMS Implementation Guide) is the most detailed official source of personal guidance. It covers caffeine timing, naps, sleep environment, recovery sleep, light and jet lag, and it gives crew a duty to use rest to sleep and to report fatigue. It says outright that the ICAO SARPs apply to cabin crew but that its examples are built around flight crew, because the cabin crew evidence base is thin. No ICAO or IATA guidance written specifically for cabin crew self-management was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Scope and cabin crew caveat:** "The ICAO SARPs apply to both flight and cabin crew. However to date, flight crew fatigue has received much more scientific, operational, and regulatory attention than cabin crew fatigue, so the examples in this manual focus on flight crew… some mitigation strategies may be different. More specific advice on managing cabin crew fatigue will become possible as research and fatigue management experience with cabin crew increases." — [FMG for Airline Operators, 2015, "Use of this Manual"](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Edition history:** The FMG 2015 is the renamed 2nd edition of the July 2011 IATA/ICAO/IFALPA "FRMS Implementation Guide for Operators". It covers both prescriptive and FRMS approaches. Appendix A reproduces the "ICAO Fatigue Management SARPs for Flight and Cabin Crew" (Annex 6 Part I, section 4.10, section 2.1.2 and Appendix 7). — [FMG 2015, Executive Letter and Appendix A](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Two kinds of personal mitigation:**
  - The FMG splits personal mitigations into "strategic countermeasures (for use at home, for example good sleep habits, napping before night duty)" and "operational countermeasures, for example strategic use of caffeine" (Glossary).
  - Table 4-1 lists hazards with personal strategies. Examples:
    - For difficulty sleeping in onboard crew rest: "Use eye mask, ear plugs, arrange a suitable wakeup call. Avoid caffeine for 3-4 hours before trying to sleep. Strategic use of caffeine after in-flight rest period."
    - For noisy layover hotels: the same advice, plus "Submit a hazard report identifying fatigue as a contributing factor."
    - For unpredictable call-outs: "When feeling sleepy while waiting for call-out, attempt sleep (prioritize sleep over other activities)"; "Maximize recovery sleep on off-duty days."
  - — [FMG 2015, section 4.1.1, Table 4-1](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Caffeine (Operational Implication 3):**
  - "Caffeine takes approximately 30 minutes to have an effect and can last for up to 5 hours (but people differ widely…)."
  - It "does not remove the need for sleep and it should only be used as a short term strategy."
  - "caffeine should be avoided when alertness is high, such as at the beginning of a duty period, and instead used at times when sleepiness is expected to be high, e.g., towards the end of a long duty period or at the times in the circadian body clock cycle when sleepiness is greater."
  - It can also be taken "in advance of a period that is likely to be associated with higher fatigue (e.g., the early hours of the morning)."
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Alcohol and caffeine near bedtime:** Caffeine close to bedtime gives "lighter and more disturbed sleep" even in heavy coffee drinkers. While alcohol is being metabolised ("about one standard drink per hour… the brain cannot obtain REM sleep"), and REM rebound later in the night disturbs sleep. — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Sleep environment for hotel and layover sleep:**
  - "Bright light increases alertness; it is much easier to sleep in a dark room. Heavy curtains or a mask can be used."
  - White noise can mask sudden sounds.
  - A cooler room helps sleep onset: "For most people (18-20 °C / 64-68 °F) is an ideal room temperature for sleep."
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Naps (Operational Implication 5):**
  - "some sleep is always better than none."
  - Napping before an evening or night duty "will reduce the period of wakefulness," and "napping prior to work does not reduce the amount of sleep obtained during a rest break at work."
  - In-duty naps must allow time to fall asleep and time for sleep inertia to clear.
  - "It is also critical that individuals are educated to not reduce their sleep time in anticipation of a nap during a flight or duty period."
  - Napping "should not be used as a means of extending a duty period."
  - The "afternoon nap window" is "usually around 15:00-17:00", described as "a good time to try to nap" but also a time of more likely unintentional micro-sleeps.
  - — [FMG 2015, Glossary and section 2.1](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Recovery sleep:**
  - "Lost sleep is not recovered hour-for-hour."
  - "At least two consecutive nights of unrestricted sleep are required for the non-REM/REM sleep cycle to return to normal." Recovery is slower if sleep is not at night or the crew member is not adapted to local time.
  - "A 48-hour break starting at midnight will not allow most people two consecutive nights of unrestricted sleep… a 40-hour break starting at 20:00 will allow most people two consecutive nights."
  - "One 10-hour sleep opportunity at night is not enough to recover from… 5 nights of sleep restricted to 4 hours."
  - — [FMG 2015, sections 2.2.3 and Operational Implication 6](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Split sleep:** Laboratory studies suggest that a restricted night sleep plus a daytime nap has recovery value equivalent to the same total sleep in one block. The caveat is that these were lab conditions with participants adapted to local time. Split sleep also shortens continuous time awake. — [FMG 2015, section 2.2.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Light and the body clock:**
  - "light exposure after the circadian temperature low point in the morning will result in a phase advance."
  - "light exposure in the middle of the day will have minimal effect."
  - "light exposure in the evening before the circadian temperature low point will result in phase delay."
  - "the clock is particularly sensitive to blue light."
  - Exercise also shifts the clock.
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Early starts:** "The evening wake maintenance zone occurs in the few hours before usual bedtime. This makes it very difficult to fall asleep earlier than usual, ahead of an early duty report time. Early report times have been identified as a cause of restricted sleep." — [FMG 2015, Operational Implication 7](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Night duty:** Night duty "should be scheduled to end as early as possible and individuals need to get to sleep as soon as possible after coming off duty." Napping before and during night duty is recommended. — [FMG 2015, Operational Implication 7](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Standby and on-call sleep:** Sleep while on call is worse even if no call comes. Lab subjects told they might be woken took longer to fall asleep and spent longer awake. Ships' engineers on call had shorter sleep with less slow-wave and REM sleep. The guide recommends minimum notification periods and predictable standby timing. — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.3 and Operational Implication 4](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Time awake:** In an NTSB study of 37 accidents (1978–1990), crews with high time since awakening (median about 13.8 h for captains) made about 40% more errors than low-TSA crews (12.2 vs 8.7). — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.4](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Crew duties under an FRMS policy (model text):** "Flight and cabin crew are required to:"
  - "make appropriate use of their time off… to obtain sleep";
  - "participate in fatigue risk management education and training";
  - "report fatigue-related hazards and incidents";
  - "inform their manager or supervisor immediately prior to or during work if they know or suspect they or another crew member are suffering from unacceptable levels of fatigue."
  - — [FMG 2015, Chapter 6 example policy](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Possible error in the FMG's NASA citation:** The FMG says "For two-pilot crews on long range flights, planned 40-minute nap opportunities on the flight deck seat have been shown to provide an average of 23 minutes of sleep." It cites NASA TM 108839, but that study used three-person B747 crews and reports 25.8 min (see section 2). — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.4](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf) vs [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)

### Inferences
- The FMG gives an app an authoritative basis for these rules:
  - caffeine: about 30 min onset, up to 5 h duration, avoid in the 3–4 h before sleep, use it at expected lows rather than at the start of duty;
  - a pre-night-duty nap;
  - a dark, cool (18–20 °C) room;
  - two consecutive nights of unrestricted sleep for recovery;
  - morning light advances the clock and evening light delays it.
- Citing "ICAO Doc 9966" for these specific self-help rules is weaker than citing the FMG. Doc 9966 is a manual for State regulators overseeing fatigue management. The FMG is the operator- and crew-facing companion.
- Because the FMG says its examples focus on flight crew, the app should present its advice as general fatigue science applied to cabin crew, not as cabin-crew-validated guidance.

### Gaps
- The text of ICAO Doc 9966 (2nd edition 2016) was not retrieved. I could not confirm whether a later edition or revision exists as of 2026.
- No ICAO or IATA guidance document specific to cabin crew fatigue self-management was found. ICAO Doc 10002 (Cabin Crew Safety Training Manual) and IATA cabin operations guidance were not checked.

## 2. NASA Ames Fatigue Countermeasures Program: Rosekind et al. 1994 planned cockpit rest study and crew strategies

### Takeaway
NASA TM 108839 (Sept 1994; also DOT/FAA/92/24) studied 21 pilots on three-person B747 transpacific crews, not cabin crew. With a 40-minute rest opportunity, pilots slept on 93% of opportunities, fell asleep in 5.6 min and slept 25.8 min on average. Compared with the no-rest group, they had fewer PVT lapses and fewer EEG/EOG micro-events through descent and landing. The widely quoted "34% performance / 54% alertness" figures do not appear in the TM's summary.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation:** Rosekind MR, Graeber RC, Dinges DF, Connell LJ, Rountree MS, Spinweber CL, Gillen KA. "Crew Factors in Flight Operations IX: Effects of Planned Cockpit Rest on Crew Performance and Alertness in Long-Haul Operations." NASA TM 108839 / DOT/FAA/92/24, September 1994. Co-sponsored by the FAA, with Northwest and United Airlines. — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Design:**
  - "Twenty-one volunteer pilots… randomly assigned to either a rest group (N = 12) or a no-rest group (N = 9)."
  - The rest group had "a planned 40 min. rest period during the low-workload, cruise portion of flight over water. Pilots rested one at a time… with two crewmembers maintaining the flight at all times."
  - The study covered the four consecutive middle legs of a 12-day transpacific trip: two westbound day flights and two eastbound night flights.
  - The aircraft were B-747s with three-person crews.
  - — [NASA TM 108839, Operational Summary](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Rest protocol:**
  - First choice of rest went to the landing pilot.
  - Rest "ended 1 hr. before descent."
  - Only one crew member rested at a time.
  - Structure: "initial preparation period (3 min.), followed by the 40 min. rest period, followed by a recovery period (20 min.)."
  - The resting pilot was "fully briefed before reentering the operational loop."
  - — [NASA TM 108839, Operational Summary](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Sleep obtained:**
  - "on 93% of the rest period opportunities the RG pilots were able to sleep… fell asleep quickly (average = 5.6 min.) and slept for an average of 26 min." The report documentation page gives "sleeping for 25.8 minutes."
  - Night flights produced more deep (slow-wave) sleep and day flights more light sleep.
  - — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Unplanned sleep in the control group:** "four NRG pilots fell asleep (a total of five episodes) for periods lasting from several minutes to over 10 min" while instructed to keep working. The authors say this "reinforces previous findings that pilots are poor evaluators of their level of physiological sleepiness." — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **PVT performance:**
  - In-flight trials: "the NRG (with fewer subjects) had a total of 124 lapses, whereas the RG had a total of 81." A lapse is a response delay over 0.5 s.
  - The no-rest group showed a steady rise in median reaction time across legs. The body of the report states "the NRG was 10%-16% slower than the RG during the in-flight trials."
  - Before top of descent on night leg 4, "the number of lapses in the NRG pilots increased twice as much as in the RG pilots."
  - — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Physiological alertness in the last ~90 min of flight:**
  - Micro-events (more than 5 s) occurred in "78% of the NRG and 50% of the RG."
  - Totals were "120 microevents… in the NRG… and… 34 microevents in the RG."
  - Means per crewmember were 6.37 (no-rest) vs 2.90 (rest).
  - — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Subjective ratings:** "the nap did not affect the subjective ratings of alertness, though the objective measures clearly indicated better performance and greater alertness in the RG." — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Layover sleep and sleep debt:**
  - "86% of the 21 subjects accumulated a sleep debt that ranged from 4 to 22 hr. and averaged approximately 9 hr. by the ninth day."
  - Layover sleep took up 28% of time vs 33% at home.
  - "77% of the layovers involved more than one sleep episode." If the first episode was long, the second was short, and vice versa.
  - "the cockpit nap did not significantly alter the cumulative sleep debt."
  - — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **NASA's countermeasure framework:**
  - Preventive strategies: e.g., maximising sleep quantity and quality, and bright light or exercise to shift the clock, listed as possibilities for research.
  - Operational countermeasures: "judicious use of caffeine, increased physical activity, and increased interaction."
  - Napping is classed as an "acute, short-acting" operational countermeasure that does not repay a sleep debt.
  - — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Limitations stated by the authors:** one trip pattern on one carrier; only four middle legs studied, so the most fatigued final legs were not studied; "B-747 aircraft flown by three-person crews; the specific application of this countermeasure to the two-person cockpit was not addressed"; observers on the flight deck. — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **Popular claim not in the TM:** Popular sources say the "26-minute nap improved performance by 34% and alertness by 54%" ([Sleep Review](https://sleepreviewmag.com/sleep-health/sleep-whole-body/weight/facing-down-fatigue/); search snippet). These percentages do not appear in the TM's summary or conclusions. They may come from a later Rosekind summary ([Rosekind et al. 1995, J Sleep Res, "Alertness management: strategic naps in operational settings"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2869.1995.tb00229.x)); not verified.
- **Multi-trip circadian drift (NASA data reproduced in the FMG):**
  - On a multi-leg transpacific B747 pattern, crew temperature minimum "delayed by about 4.5 hours… an average drift rate of about 30 minutes per 24 hours."
  - On returning home, body clocks "were on average 4.5 hours delayed… and took several days to readapt."
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.5, citing Gander et al. 1998](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)

### Inferences
- What the study did show:
  - Sleep-deprived pilots fall asleep fast (5.6 min is "comparable to… moderately sleep deprived individuals").
  - A short, planned, protected nap with a 20-minute wake-up buffer improves objective alertness at top of descent.
  - Subjective ratings miss this.
- What it did not show:
  - any data on cabin crew, galley workload or cabin bunks;
  - two-pilot crews;
  - long-term effects on sleep debt (there were none).
- The app should therefore:
  - cite it as "pilot flight-deck study";
  - use it to justify short in-flight naps of about 40 min with time to recover from sleep inertia;
  - not quote "34%/54%" unless sourced to the 1995 paper.
- The finding that perceived sleepiness misses objective impairment argues against relying only on self-rated fatigue in the app.

### Gaps
- NASA's crew education module ("Strategies for Alertness Management", NASA TM-1996-110404 / Rosekind et al. 1996) and the "anchor sleep" concept were not retrieved. I could not quote NASA's exact layover and anchor-sleep recommendations.

## 3. Cabin crew fatigue evidence: prevalence, causes, key studies

### Takeaway
The best quantitative cabin crew evidence is the FAA CAMI work. In a 9,180-respondent US survey (2009), 84% reported fatigue on duty in the previous bid period and 52% reported having "nodded off" on a flight. The causes rated most fatiguing were duty days of 14 h or more, quick turns under 9 h, 10–13 h duty days, long runs of consecutive duty days, short layovers, no breaks and missed meals. An actigraphy field study (202 FAs, 2010) measured 5.7 h of sleep on workdays, and 4.9 h in international operations. The EASA 2019 FTL study found high fatigue risk on night duties and late finishes for both pilots and cabin crew.

### Cited Findings
- **CAMI Part I (Avers, King, Nesthus, Thomas, Banks), DOT/FAA/AM-09/24, Dec 2009:**
  - Sample: 9,180 US flight attendants from 30 operators (17 regional, 7 low-cost, 6 network). Surveys went to 22,594 people.
  - Demographics: 79% female, mean age 45.9. — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Prevalence and safety impact (CAMI Part I):**
  - "84% reported being fatigued in their previous bid period. More than half… (52%) indicated they had 'nodded off' while working on a flight segment/leg."
  - Of those fatigued, 83% blamed work schedule, 44% workload and 42% work pace.
  - Of fatigued FAs, "71% reported their safety-related performance was affected":
    - 60% said responding to passenger needs was compromised;
    - 36% said cabin safety tasks (e.g., arming/disarming doors) were affected;
    - 34% said security vigilance was affected;
    - 14% said preflight safety briefings were affected.
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Top-10 fatigue factors by rated effect (CAMI Part I):** "14 hr or longer duty day, quick shift turnaround (less than 9 hours), 10 to 13-hr duty day, 14 or more consecutive duty days, short layovers, no breaks, missed meals, long delays (3 hr or more), missed breaks, and 8-13 consecutive duty days."
  - Effect means: ≥14 h duty day 4.19; quick turns 3.82; 10–13 h duty 3.77.
  - Of these, the 10–13 h duty day, missed meals, no breaks and short layovers occur more often than "occasionally."
  - Early-morning report (effect 3.56, frequency 3.43), continuous duty overnights, jet lag (3.47), poor-nutrition meals and flying 4 or more legs (3.45) also contributed "to a moderate extent or greater."
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Duty patterns (CAMI Part I):**
  - Averages: 3.9 duty days per 7 days; 9.6 h per duty day (range 6.4–12.9); 6.8 flight hours; 2.8 legs per day (max average 4).
  - Reports were most often at 04:00–07:59 (mean 6.0 times per bid period). Duty ends were most often at 20:00–23:59 (mean 5.1).
  - 27% never had the chance to leave the aircraft between legs. 93% had delays, with a typical delay of 54 min.
  - "it took flight attendants an average 49 min to arrive at their rest accommodations" after the door opened, including an average 16 min wait for transport.
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Sleep at home vs away (CAMI Part I):**
  - Home: 7.7 h per night.
  - Away: 6.5 h per night.
  - 90% reported problems falling asleep away from home, on an average of 6 nights per bid period.
  - 39% used a sleep aid away from home: 18% prescription, 24% over-the-counter, 17% alcohol, 8% melatonin.
  - Top factors disturbing sleep away: random noise (3.35), "fear of oversleeping" (3.21), temperature (3.03), jet lag (3.00).
  - Away-from-home sleep quality: 34% poor or very poor vs 10% at home.
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Commuting, reserve and continuous duty overnights (CAMI Part I):**
  - Commuting: 38% commuted more than 1.5 h, and 28% commuted by air.
  - Reserve: 22% flew reserve in the bid period. 43% got only 1–2 h notice before report time, and 11% had 4 h or less of sleep before reporting.
  - Continuous duty overnights (CDOs): 30% flew at least one CDO, and 52% rated rest on CDOs poor or very poor.
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Coping and crew requests (CAMI Part I):**
  - Common coping strategies included nutritious meals, water, vitamins, naps, walks, multiple meals and caffeine. 90% drank at least one caffeinated beverage per 24 h.
  - The three most-requested changes were "Begin rest period on arrival at hotel, avoid multiple-hour breaks between flights, and provide food and drink on flights."
  - Only 9% felt airlines adjusted schedules to minimise fatigue to a considerable or great extent.
  - — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **CAMI field study (Flight Attendant Fatigue Recommendation II, AM-10/22, 2010):**
  - 202 US FAs (network, low-cost, regional) wore actigraphs and did PVT tests for 3–4 weeks.
  - "flight attendants slept 6.3 hr on days off and 5.7 hr on work days, fell asleep 29 min after going to bed, awoke four times per sleep episode, and spent 77% of each episode actually sleeping."
  - "Those working international operations slept significantly less (4.9 hr vs. 5.9 hr) and less efficiently (75% vs. 79%)."
  - "All flight attendants exhibited significant impairments during pre-work PVT tests when compared to their own optimum baseline."
  - — [CAMI AM-10/22](https://libraryonline.erau.edu/online-full-text/faa-aviation-medicine-reports/AM10-22.pdf)
- **EASA "Effectiveness of FTL" study (28 Feb 2019):**
  - Found "an increased probability of high fatigue levels, especially during nights and duty periods with late finishes, among both pilots and cabin crew."
  - For nights, predictors were "encroachment on the window of circadian low (WOCL… 2 am and 5.59 am)" and "short prior sleep."
  - It made six recommendations, including promoting "more decisively the use of resting opportunities in the context of night duties."
  - — [EASA news, 2019](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/newsroom-and-events/news/easa-report-effectiveness-flight-time-limitation-recommends-enhancing)
- **EASA safety report, 2026 (secondary):** It identified aircrew fatigue and communication issues as "the most consistent safety precursors." — [ETF summary (search snippet only)](https://www.etf-europe.org/easa-report-highlights-fatigue-and-communication-issues-as-key-aviation-safety-concerns/)
- **Scoping review:** A 2023 scoping review, "Fatigue and Sleep in Airline Cabin Crew", exists. — [PMC9916232](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9916232/) (full text blocked by captcha; contents not verified)

### Inferences
- The patterns the app should model as cabin-crew-specific fatigue drivers are:
  - early reports (04:00–07:59);
  - multi-sector days with no breaks or meals;
  - quick turns;
  - poor hotel sleep from noise, fear of oversleeping and temperature;
  - reserve with short call-out notice.
- "Fear of oversleeping" ranking second among hotel sleep disruptors suggests a reliable alarm-and-wake plan is a real user need.
- The 49-minute door-to-hotel average shows that "rest period" is not "sleep opportunity". The app should subtract transport, check-in, eating and wind-down time.

### Gaps
- No Indian (IndiGo, Air India, Akasa) or Gulf (Emirates, Etihad, Qatar) cabin crew fatigue studies were retrieved. The Europe PMC query failed and there were no further searches. This is an important gap for the primary market.
- The full CAMI Part II–VI series (literature review, incident reports, international regulation comparison, countermeasure training) was not reviewed.
- The full EASA 2019 report (sample sizes, cabin crew data) and the 2026 EASA report were not read.

## 4. Layover strategy evidence: home-base time vs adapting to local time; east vs west; ULR

### Takeaway
The FMG says crew "rarely have enough time in a destination to adapt fully" (1–2 day layovers are typical). With 1–2 day layovers there is "very little circadian adaptation." Crew therefore typically split layover sleep between local night and home-time night. Westward adaptation is faster than eastward. After eastward trips of 6 or more zones the clock may shift the "wrong" way. Regulators encode the same idea: DGCA treats crew as acclimatised to the departure time zone for the first 48 h.

### Cited Findings
- **Adaptation on short layovers:**
  - "Crew members who operate transmeridian flights rarely have enough time in a destination to adapt fully to local time, with 1-2 day layovers being typical."
  - "there appears to be very little circadian adaptation across flights leaving and returning to a crew member's domicile time zone, with a 1-2 day layover."
  - Longer back-to-back transmeridian sequences can push the clock towards a non-24-hour period.
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.5](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Split layover sleep:** "Often, crew members split their sleep, having one sleep period on local night and another corresponding to local night in their home time zone, which overlaps the preferred part of the circadian body clock cycle for sleep (at least for the first 24-48 hours in a new time zone)." — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.5](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Direction and other factors:**
  - "Adaptation is usually faster after westward travel (phase delay) than after eastward travel."
  - "After eastward flights across 6 or more time zones, the circadian body clock may adapt by shifting in the opposite direction… adaptation can be particularly slow."
  - "Adaptation is faster when the circadian body clock is more exposed to local time cues, including outdoor light, and exercising and eating on local time."
  - "Beginning a trip with a sleep debt seems to increase the duration and severity of jet lag symptoms."
  - — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.5](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Extended stays in the destination region:** When crew stay several days in the destination region, their clocks begin to adapt and layover sleep may improve, but "when they arrive back in their home time zone, they may need additional days to readapt." — [FMG 2015, citing Gander et al. 2013, Chronobiol Int 30:963-972](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Wake time on unaugmented long-haul:** One study found that "the average duty day involved staying awake for 20.6 hours (the average duty period lasted 9.8 hours)." — [FMG 2015, section 2.3.5](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **NASA layover data:** 77% of layovers had more than one sleep episode, and the episodes were compensatory (long then short, or short then long). This reflects "multiple factors operating to control sleep timing… (e.g., local time, home circadian time, prior sleep loss)." — [NASA TM 108839](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19950006379/downloads/19950006379.pdf)
- **DGCA acclimatisation rules:**
  - Draft Issue V: crew are "considered to be acclimatised to a 3-hour wide time zone surrounding the local time at the point of departure… considered to be acclimatised to the departure time zone for the first 48 hour. After 48 hours… acclimatized to the local time where the crew member starts his/her next duty."
  - Issue IV WOCL definition: the home-base WOCL applies "for the first 48 hours only," and after more than 48 hours away, the WOCL is referenced to local time.
  - — [DGCA draft CAR 7-J-I Issue V, para 3.1](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US); [CAR 7-J-I Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)
- **UAE acclimatisation:** GCAA defines acclimatised as having spent "3 consecutive local nights on the ground within a local time zone band, which is two hours wide." — [GCAA CAR-OPS 1.1110 (2017 copy)](https://understandinggcaafdtl.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/gcaa-subpart-q-2017.pdf)
- **ULR recovery (van den Berg et al.):** Cabin crew ULR research supports "a minimum rest period that includes at least four local nights following a ULR trip." In the same work, "a longer period awake was associated with higher fatigue ratings at top of descent." — [van den Berg et al. via Frontiers (search snippet)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-health/articles/10.3389/fenvh.2023.1329206/full)

### Inferences
- A sound default rule for the app:
  - Layovers of up to about 48 h (DGCA's own 48-hour threshold) or 1–2 local nights: stay on home-base timing. Anchor the main sleep in home-night hours, nap in the local night, and avoid strong light at times that would shift the clock.
  - Layovers of 3 or more local nights (the GCAA threshold) or extended regional flying: partially adapt.
- The app's "fixed WOCL" should be replaced by a WOCL that follows acclimatisation state:
  - home-base 02:00–05:59 (Issue IV) or 02:00–06:00 (draft Issue V) for the first 48 h away;
  - destination-referenced after that.
- Eastward trips of 6 or more zones deserve more caution and a longer recovery estimate.
- These are inferred rules of thumb. No primary study comparing "stay on home time vs adapt" for layovers under 48 h was retrieved.

### Gaps
- The experimental literature on retaining home-base sleep hours was not retrieved (e.g., Lowden & Åkerstedt 1998, home-base sleep on a westward 9-zone trip). The Waterhouse et al. Lancet 2007 jet lag review and NASA anchor-sleep guidance were also not retrieved.
- Primary ULR guidelines were not retrieved (Flight Safety Foundation 2005 ULR guidelines, the Qantas Project Sunrise 2019 research flights).

## 5. In-flight rest for cabin crew (bunks, rest seats, split duty) and its effectiveness

### Takeaway
Regulators tie cabin crew FDP extensions to the quality of the rest facility. A bunk earns more extension than a rest seat. Cabin crew get less in-flight rest than pilots because of meal services. In ULR flights, cabin crew sleepiness at top of descent tracked workload more than in-flight sleep. Precise cabin crew in-flight sleep amounts were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- **ULR workload vs rest:**
  - "subjective sleepiness and performance at top of descent [were] more strongly associated with workload ratings than with how much sleep cabin crew obtained in flight or how long they had been awake."
  - "cabin crew have less time available for in-flight rest compared to flight crew because they are required to be awake during meal services."
  - — [Massey University news on van den Berg et al.](https://www.massey.ac.nz/about/news/perceived-workload-contributes-to-cabin-crew-fatigue/) and [PsyPost summary](https://www.psypost.org/workload-is-an-important-factor-contributing-to-airline-cabin-crew-fatigue-on-ultra-long-range-flights/) (search snippets)
- **Pilot evidence on in-flight sleep:** Sleep in onboard crew rest is lighter and more fragmented than sleep on the ground. The FMG cites Signal, Gander & van den Berg 2004, "Sleep in flight during long rest opportunities." — [FMG 2015, section 2.1.3](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **DGCA Issue IV (in force), para 7.6:**
  - For continuous flight time beyond 11 h, a Senior Cabin Crew must always be on duty during the other's rest.
  - "For flights with flight time greater than 11 hours or flight duty period greater than 15 hours, cabin crew shall be augmented by 50% of the minimum crew requirement."
  - "The minimum in-flight rest shall be not less than Flight Time minus 11 hrs."
  - Rest must be "in a comfortable horizontal seat, or in a bunk, separated and screened from the passengers."
  - — [CAR 7-J-I Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)
- **DGCA draft Issue V rest facility definitions:**
  - Bunk: reclines at least 80° from vertical, separate from the cabin.
  - Isolated Rest Seat: at least 45° recline, at least 55 in pitch, at least 20 in width, curtained.
  - Basic Rest Seat: at least 40° recline, curtained, not next to passengers.
- **DGCA draft Issue V augmented FDP table (para 7.1):**

  | Flight time | Bunk | Isolated rest seat | Basic rest seat | Landings |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | 12 h | 17 h | 15 h | 14 h | 2 |
  | 14 h | 18 h | 16 h | n/a | 1 |
  | ULR, above 14 h, max flight time 17 h | 21 h | n/a | n/a | 1 |

- **DGCA draft Issue V augmentation and ULR rest:**
  - Augmentation is 40% for flight time over 10 h or FDP over 15 h.
  - Minimum in-flight rest is "Flight Time minus 10 hrs till FDP of 14 hrs."
  - ULR: horizontal bunks are required, and the in-flight rest plan must give "at least one (1) rest period… not less than four (4) hours."
  - — [DGCA draft CAR 7-J-I Issue V, paras 3.5–3.14, 7.1, 9.6, 13](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US)
- **GCAA (UAE) in-flight relief:**
  - Total in-flight rest under 3 h gives no FDP extension.
  - At 3 h or more, a bunk or flat-bed seat extends the FDP by half the rest taken, to a maximum FDP of "18 hours or 19 hours in the case of Cabin Crew Member." A reclining seat (more than 40°) extends it by one third, to a maximum of "15 hours or 16 hours" for cabin crew.
  - The GCAA may accept, on application, reserved reclining seats in a cabin-crew-only row.
  - Long Range Operations (more than 7 h sectors): "Cabin crew should be provided with a minimum in flight rest period of (3 1/2) hours for any LRO flight", with a cabin crew complement of doors × 1.5.
  - — [GCAA CAR-OPS 1.1127(b) and AMC3 to 1.1120(d) (2017 copy)](https://understandinggcaafdtl.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/gcaa-subpart-q-2017.pdf)
- **Qatar, 2020 (pandemic period):** The QCAA reportedly allowed cabin crew to work continuous out-and-back long-haul flights with no layover if given "onboard 'horizontal' rest of at least five hours" in crew bunks. — [Paddle Your Own Kanoo, 22 Jun 2020 (search snippet)](https://www.paddleyourownkanoo.com/2020/06/22/reports-qatar-airways-cabin-crew-working-long-haul-flights-with-no-layover-and-no-crew-change/)
- **Split duty (DGCA Issue IV and draft V):**
  - A break under 3 h earns no extension. A break of 3–10 h extends the FDP by half the break. A break over 10 h earns none.
  - "Suitable accommodation" is required if the break is over 6 h or encroaches on the WOCL.
  - The FDP parts before and after the break must each be no more than 10 h.
  - Draft V adds: "No extension shall be permitted if FDP encroaches night duty period."
  - — [DGCA Issue IV para 6.4](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf); [draft V para 8.6](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US)
- **CAMI on breaks:** 24% of US FAs had scheduled breaks in the duty day (average 82 min). Of CDO FAs with onboard rest, 95% had dedicated facilities. — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)

### Inferences
- The app should treat in-flight rest as a real but partial sleep opportunity. A 3–4 h bunk break might yield a fraction of that as sleep, but no cabin-crew-specific figure was found.
- In-flight rest windows should be planned around service phases.
- The "take caffeine after, not within 3–4 h before, the rest break" rule from the FMG applies.
- The roster parser should capture the rest facility class (bunk vs seat), the crew complement and the break position, because these drive both the legal FDP and the realistic sleep opportunity.

### Gaps
- The exact sleep amounts from van den Berg et al. 2015 ("Monitoring and managing cabin crew sleep and fatigue during an ultra-long range trip," Aerosp Med Hum Perform 86(8):705-713) were not retrieved.
- Sammito et al. 2022 (ULR flight attendants, Health Behavior and Policy Review / Liebert) was not reviewed.
- EASA CS FTL.1.205 in-flight rest minimums for cabin crew by rest facility class were not retrieved.

### Takeaway

### Cited Findings
#### India: CAR 7-J-I Issue IV (in force)
Source: third-party transcription of the CAR; the official DGCA PDF was not retrieved.
- **Title and scope:** "DGCA – CAR – 7 – J – I (Issue IV), Effective 01-Nov-2018." It applies to all cabin crew of Scheduled, Non-Scheduled and Scheduled Commuter Air Transport Services. — [Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf); also stated by [Wingmanlog](https://www.wingmanlog.in/post/understanding-cabin-crew-fdtl-guidelines-dgca-car-7-j-i)
- **Definitions:**
  - WOCL is "between 02:00 and 05:59". It is home-base time for FDPs crossing 3 or fewer zones, and for the first 48 h beyond 3 zones. After more than 48 h away it is referenced to local time at the point of departure.
  - Local night is "eight hours falling between 2200 hours and 0800 hours".
  - Post-flight allowance (part of the duty period) is "15 minutes" for non-scheduled and commuter services, "30 minutes for Scheduled Air Transport Services", and "60 minutes in case of International Operations where the cabin crew are required to undertake post flight medical examination."
  - FDP runs from report until the engines are shut down after the last flight. "Each operator shall prescribe in their operations manual the reporting time for cabin crew."

- **Daily maximums (para 6.1):**

  | Flight time | Max FDP | Landings |
  |---|---|---|
  | 8 h or less | 12:00 | 6 |
  | over 8 h, up to 11 h | 15:00 | 3 |
  | over 11 h, up to 16 h | 18:00 | 1 |
  | over 16 h (ULR) | 22:00 | 1, case-by-case DGCA approval |

  - WOCL reduction: "When the FDP starts in the WOCL, the maximum FDP… shall be reduced by 100% of its encroachment upto a maximum of 2 hours. When the FDP ends in or fully encompasses the WOCL… reduced by 50% of its encroachment."
  - Note: the transcription's ">11 and ≤16" band conflicts with its own ULR definition (over 14 h). Wingmanlog lists the 18:00 band as 11–14 h. Verify against the official DGCA PDF.
- **Cumulative limits:**
  - Flight time: 40:00 in 7 days, 115:00 in 28 days, 300:00 in 90 days, 1000:00 in 365 days.
  - Duty: 65:00 in 7 days, 210:00 in 28 days.
- **Minimum rest before an FDP (para 7.1):** the greater of the preceding duty period or 12 h within 3 zones; 14 h when crossing 3 zones; 36 h when crossing 7 zones.
  - Positioning: if the preceding duty including positioning exceeds 18 h, the rest must include a local night.
  - Transport to and from the airport counts as neither duty nor rest.
- **Weekly rest:** "continuous 36 hours including two local night," with no more than 168 h between weekly rests, at home base.
- **Rest on return to home base after WOCL encroachment:**
  - over 3 and up to 7 zones: 24 h with 1 local night if WOCL encroachment is 50% or less; 36 h with 2 local nights if more than 50%;
  - over 7 zones: 72 h with 3 local nights;
  - plus 12 h for ULR.
- **Standby:**
  - Maximum 12 h, of which at most 8 h at the airport.
  - Airport standby counts 100% towards FDP if it leads to a flight.
  - Home or hotel standby: none counts if called within the first 6 h; at 6 h or later the FDP is reduced by 50% of the standby time; if not called, 25% counts as duty.
  - Rest after uncalled standby: 8 h for home standby, 12 h for airport standby.
  - Standby must be notified at least 12 h ahead.
- **Positioning:** All positioning time counts as duty. It is part of the FDP if it immediately precedes one, but it is not an operating sector.
- **Consecutive nights:** No flight duty "embracing 00:00 to 05:00 hours local time for two consecutive nights except once within a period of 168 hours."
- **Extensions for unforeseen circumstances:**
  - Flight time up to +1:30; FDP up to +4 h.
  - Cumulative limits of 3 h flight time and 10 h FDP per 28 days.
  - Rest increases by twice the extension.
  - The Senior Cabin Crew conveys crew consent to the PIC.
- **Reserve:** Reserve counts as neither duty nor rest.
- **Fatigue duties:** The operator "shall not require" a cabin crew member who is "known or suspected" to be fatigued to operate. Crew "shall plan and use rest periods to ensure that they are fully rested" and shall not operate if fatigued.

- — [Federal Register 2022-21963](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2022-10-12/pdf/2022-21963.pdf); [Cozen O'Connor](https://www.cozen.com/news-resources/publications/2022/faa-issues-final-rule-increasing-mandatory-flight-attendant-rest-periods)

### Inferences
- The app's FDP compliance check must:
  - be versioned (DGCA Issue IV now, with draft V possibly coming);
  - be carrier- and regulator-specific (DGCA vs GCAA vs QCAA/EASA);
  - avoid presenting itself as a legal determination.
- It should model the following, all of which change legal limits and sleep opportunity:
  - acclimatisation state;
  - the post-flight allowance (30 or 60 min);
  - the consecutive-nights rule;
  - standby type and timing;
  - positioning;
  - return-to-base rest.
- A fixed WOCL is wrong in two ways:
  - The window differs by rule: DGCA Issue IV 02:00–05:59, draft V and pilot CAR 02:00–06:00, GCAA night duty 02:00–04:59.
  - The reference time zone depends on acclimatisation.

### Gaps

- I could not confirm whether the GCAA has replaced Subpart Q since 2017 (e.g., with an EASA-style CAR-AIR OPS).

  - the CS FTL.1.205 cabin crew in-flight rest table.
  
  These are known from background knowledge only; verify before use.
- The FA Fatigue Risk Management Plan requirement (PL 111-216 and section 336 of the 2018 Act) was not verified.

## 7. Airline-side fatigue tools (FRMS, biomathematical models), crew interaction, reporting culture, and fit of a consumer app

### Takeaway
Within ICAO, FMG and DGCA frameworks, fatigue management is a shared responsibility. Crew must use rest to sleep and report fatigue, and operators must run non-punitive reporting. Biomathematical models (SAFTE-FAST, BAM, CAS, FAID) are tools within operator FRMS and rostering; they are not regulatory limits. An independent app fits best as a "strategic and operational personal countermeasure" planner that encourages formal fatigue reporting. It should never act as a fitness-for-duty decision tool.

### Cited Findings
- **FRMS and shared responsibility:** The FRMS policy must "reflect the shared responsibility of management, flight and cabin crews." Crew must report fatigue hazards and tell supervisors if fatigued. — [FMG 2015, Ch. 6 and Appendix A (Annex 6 Appendix 7)](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Models in the FMG:** The FMG discusses "Bio-mathematical Model Thresholds as SPIs" (section 5.4.3) and the use of crew fatigue reports, surveys, sleep monitoring (actigraphy) and PVT (Appendix B). — [FMG 2015 TOC](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Personal mitigations and hazard reports go together:** The FMG pairs personal strategies with "Submit a hazard report identifying fatigue as a contributing factor" for recurring problems (bad hotels, tag flights, specific city pairs). — [FMG 2015, Table 4-1](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **DGCA reporting:**
  - The draft cabin crew CAR requires a fatigue report policy stating "how to raise" and "what to do". It must be "non-punitive and confidentiality", with reports kept for 1 year and quarterly reports to DGCA. — [DGCA draft Issue V, paras 4.11–4.13](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US)
  - Issue IV has no equivalent fatigue reporting clause; it has crew and operator fatigue duties only. — [Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)
- **GCAA FRMS:** FRMS is mandatory for ULR. — [GCAA CAR-OPS 1.1130](https://understandinggcaafdtl.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/gcaa-subpart-q-2017.pdf)
- **CAMI validation of a model for FAs:** CAMI's AM-10/22 field study was titled "Field Validation of Biomathematical Fatigue Modeling" for FAs (actigraphy and PVT). — [CAMI AM-10/22](https://libraryonline.erau.edu/online-full-text/faa-aviation-medicine-reports/AM10-22.pdf)
- **Crew trust in airlines:** Only 18% of US FAs felt their airline showed considerable or great concern for their health, and 9% felt schedules were adjusted to minimise fatigue. This is a trust gap an independent app could fill. — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **IATA FRMS white paper:** IATA published an FRMS white paper in 2025. — [IATA FRMS white paper 2025](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/frms_white-paper_2025.pdf) (downloaded but not reviewed in detail)

### Inferences
- To complement rather than conflict with airline systems, the app should:
  - avoid issuing "fit or unfit" verdicts or numeric alertness scores that could contradict the operator's model;
  - frame outputs as sleep and countermeasure planning;
  - prompt users to file fatigue reports through the airline's system;
  - keep data private (fatigue data is sensitive in employment contexts).
- NASA's finding that people misjudge their own sleepiness argues for an objective component, but the app should not claim model validation it lacks.

### Gaps
- No sources were gathered on:
  - SAFTE-FAST, Jeppesen BAM/CAS or FAID specifics;
  - crew-facing apps (e.g., Jeppesen CrewAlert);
  - Gulf or Indian fatigue reporting culture;
  - legal or union positions on consumer fatigue apps.

## 8. Practical realities the app must model

### Takeaway
The app must model a duty-day timeline that regulators and surveys describe consistently. That timeline runs: report (operator-defined) → FDP → chocks-on plus post-flight allowance (DGCA 30 min, 60 min for international with medical) → transport (DGCA: counts as neither duty nor rest) → hotel. The real sleep opportunity is well below the legal rest. Standby, reserve, positioning, split duty, consecutive nights and missed meals all change fatigue and legal limits.

### Cited Findings
- **Report time:** DGCA requires each operator to set cabin crew reporting time in its operations manual, covering briefing, medical (breath analyser) and admin. Draft V requires the scheme to define pre-flight times by domestic or international flight, airport layout and immigration. — [Issue IV definitions](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf); [draft V para 4.4.1](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US)
- **Debrief and post-flight:**
  - DGCA: 15, 30 or 60 min as above. — [Issue IV](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)
  - GCAA: "a minimum of 30 minutes duty will be allowed for post-flight activities." — [GCAA 1.1127(j)](https://understandinggcaafdtl.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/gcaa-subpart-q-2017.pdf)
- **Hotel transport:**
  - DGCA Issue IV: transport counts as neither duty nor rest.
  - Draft V: transport over 30 min increases rest by twice the excess.
  - GCAA: travel over 30 min each way extends rest; the room must be available for at least 10 h.
  - US data: 49 min from door-open to hotel.
  - — [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US); [GCAA](https://understandinggcaafdtl.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/03/gcaa-subpart-q-2017.pdf); [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Standby and reserve:** DGCA airport vs home/hotel standby rules (section 6). The FMG notes that anticipating a call degrades sleep, and CAMI found short reserve call-out notice. — [sources above](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Deadheading and positioning:** Counts as duty (DGCA, GCAA). If it precedes an FDP it is part of that FDP. — [DGCA Issue IV section 9](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)
- **Meals:**
  - DGCA requires crew to "make best use of facilities and opportunities… for rest and for the consumption of meals."
  - US FAs ranked missed meals and poor-nutrition meals among the top fatigue factors.
  - 51% ate only 2 meals per 24 h on duty.
  - Crew comment: airline "carb loaded food… causes drowsiness."
  - — [DGCA draft V para 5.2](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=il6xIB9QE6ehTbKQ7uUHyQ%3D%3D&baseLocale=en_US); [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf)
- **Hotel sleep:** Noise, fear of oversleeping and temperature are the main disruptors. The FMG recommends a mask, earplugs, a wake-up call and a cool room. The draft DGCA CAR's "suitable accommodation" means a separate room with control of light and temperature, and airlines are lobbying against the separate-room mandate. — [CAMI Part I](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/200924.pdf); [Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/air-india-indigo-lobby-dgca-to-ease-draft-cabin-crew-fatigue-regulations)

### Inferences
- The roster model needs these fields:
  - report time;
  - sector blocks;
  - chocks-on;
  - debrief allowance;
  - transport estimate;
  - hotel arrival;
  - wind-down;
  - planned sleep window;
  - wake buffer;
  - next report time.
- It should also capture duty type: flight, standby (home or airport), reserve, positioning, training or ground duty.

### Gaps
- Actual carrier report and debrief times for IndiGo, Air India, Akasa, Emirates, Etihad and Qatar were not found; they are set in non-public operations manuals.
- No Gulf or Indian data on hotel transport times or galley meal patterns was found.
