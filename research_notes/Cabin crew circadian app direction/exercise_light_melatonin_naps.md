# Exercise, Light, Melatonin and Napping as Circadian and Fatigue Countermeasures for Airline Cabin Crew

Evidence grades used: **strong** (multiple RCTs/meta-analyses or well-controlled lab PRCs with consistent results), **moderate** (single good RCT/lab study, or consistent smaller studies), **weak** (small, field, observational or indirect studies), **speculative** (extrapolation, no direct human evidence). Research cut-off: abstracts/full texts retrieved Oct 2026. Note: the web-search budget ran out before the regulatory and aviation-medicine questions could be verified, so most of those items are listed under Gaps.

---

## 1. Exercise as a circadian zeitgeber (phase shifting)

### Takeaway
Timed exercise shifts the human clock, but by modest amounts: roughly 0.5 h of advance after several days of morning exercise. Morning exercise (~07:00) and early-afternoon exercise (13:00–16:00) advance the clock. Evening exercise (19:00–22:00) delays it. Evening exercise works differently by chronotype. Exercise is a useful add-on to light, not a replacement. **Grade: moderate** for direction and timing. **Weak** for using it alone to treat jet lag or shift work.

### Cited Findings
- **Youngstedt, Elliott & Kripke 2019 (J Physiol)**
  - Participants: 51 older adults (59–75 y) and 48 young adults (18–30 y), aerobically fit, on a 90-min ultrashort sleep–wake cycle for up to 5.5 days.
  - Exercise: 60 min of moderate treadmill exercise (65–75% of heart-rate reserve) on 3 consecutive days, at one of 8 times of day.
  - Peak phase advances came at 07:00 and 13:00–16:00. Delays came at 19:00–22:00. Shifts were minimal around 16:00 and 02:00.
  - No age or sex differences.
  - The authors say the PRC amplitudes "are comparable to expectations for bright light of equal duration". They suggest advances for eastward travel and delays for westward travel, and say light and exercise may add together.
  - Phase was measured with urinary aMT6s, a less precise marker than plasma or salivary DLMO.
  - Sources: [Europe PMC abstract / PMC6462487](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6462487/); [J Physiol](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1113/jp276943)
- **Thomas et al. 2020 (JCI Insight)**
  - Participants: 52 sedentary adults aged 18–45 (16 men, 36 women).
  - Exercise: 30 min of treadmill exercise at 70% VO2peak for 5 days, either in the morning (10 h after DLMO) or the evening (20 h after DLMO).
  - Morning exercise advanced the clock by 0.62 ± 0.18 h. Evening exercise had no net effect (−0.02 ± 0.18 h).
  - Later chronotypes (DLMO ≥22:12) advanced with both morning exercise (0.54 h) and evening exercise (0.46 h).
  - Earlier chronotypes advanced with morning exercise (0.49 h) but delayed with evening exercise (−0.41 h).
  - Sleep duration did not differ between groups. Morning exercisers fell asleep earlier.
  - Source: [JCI Insight](https://insight.jci.org/articles/view/134270)
- **Barger et al. 2004**
  - 18 young, fit men in very dim light (~0.65 lux) after a 9-h delay of the sleep schedule.
  - Three 45-min cycling bouts each night produced significantly larger DLMO delays than in non-exercising controls.
  - The closer the exercise was to DLMO, the larger the delay (r = −0.73).
  - Source: [Am J Physiol Regul 2004, PMID 15031136](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15031136)
- **Eastman et al. 1995 (simulated night shift)**
  - 8 exercise and 8 control subjects. All wore dark welder's goggles outdoors and slept in dark bedrooms.
  - Cycling for 15 min every hour during the first 3 of 8 night shifts gave large temperature-rhythm delays (>6 h) in 63% of exercisers vs 38% of controls. The group difference was significant after adjusting for morningness–eveningness.
  - Source: [Physiol Behav, PMID 8623034](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/8623034)
- **Buxton et al. 2003**
  - Healthy men under modified constant routine (~42 lux).
  - A single 1-h bout of high-intensity exercise in the evening advanced phase by 30 ± 15 min vs −25 ± 14 min with no exercise.
  - The effect faded by the second day. The authors concluded that repeated or longer exercise may be needed for reliable shifts.
  - Source: [Am J Physiol Regul, PMID 12571075](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12571075)
- **Scale comparison with light**
  - A single 6.7-h bright-light pulse (~10,000 lux) produced a PRC with a 5.02-h peak-to-trough amplitude — [Khalsa et al. 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12717008).
  - A single 1-h pulse (~8,000 lux) produced 2.20 h — [St Hilaire et al. 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22547633).
  - Exercise studies report about 0.3–0.6 h shifts after 1–5 days of exercise ([Thomas 2020](https://insight.jci.org/articles/view/134270); [Buxton 2003](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12571075)).
- **Lewis et al. 2018 systematic review**: the evidence is compatible with exercise acting as a zeitgeber and supports timing exercise to the individual's circadian phase — [BMJ Open SEM](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/30687511).
- **Athletes and jet lag (Janse van Rensburg et al. 2020, BJSM systematic review)**
  - 22 studies were included.
  - Evidence for managing jet lag in athletes, including with exercise, was of low quality. No studies were found on travel fatigue.
  - Source: [BJSM, PMID 32303523](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32303523)

### Inferences
- **Advancing (eastward trips or early-start blocks):** suggest morning (~07:00 body-clock time) or early-afternoon (13:00–16:00) exercise windows. **Grade: moderate.**
- **Delaying (westward trips or before night blocks):** suggest evening exercise (19:00–22:00), but warn early chronotypes. **Grade: moderate** for timing, **weak** for real-world benefit.
- The Youngstedt times are clock times for people on normal schedules. A crew member's body clock may not match local time after travel, so the app should key exercise windows to an estimated body-clock (DLMO/CBTmin) time.
- Exercise should be paired with light that pushes in the same direction. Any outdoor exercise is also a light exposure, which may dominate the effect.
- Expect at most about 0.5 h/day of benefit from exercise. Frame it as "supports adaptation", not "fixes jet lag".

### Gaps
- No direct field RCTs of timed exercise for airline crew jet lag were found.
- Exercise PRCs for people who are already misaligned (for example, after a night flight) are unknown.
- The Youngstedt full text has per-time-point magnitudes in hours, but they could not be extracted (only the abstract and sentences were available).

## 2. Exercise and sleep, performance timing, and safety

### Takeaway
- Moderate evening exercise does not harm sleep. Vigorous or maximal exercise ending within about 1–2 h of bedtime can delay sleep onset and shorten sleep. The largest wearable study suggests a cutoff of ≥4 h before sleep for hard sessions.
- Peak strength and power performance is in the late afternoon and early evening (about 13:00–20:00). For training adaptation, consistency matters more than time of day.
- Sleep loss reduces exercise performance by about 7.6%, and the decline grows with hours awake.
- Exercise does not reliably cure sleep inertia.
- Post-long-haul DVT risk is real but low in absolute terms.

### Cited Findings
- **Stutz, Eiholzer & Spengler 2019 (Sports Med meta-analysis)**
  - 23 studies were included.
  - Evening exercise increased REM latency (+7.7 min) and slow-wave sleep (+1.3 percentage points), and decreased stage 1 sleep (−0.9 pp). The authors say the studies "do not support the hypothesis that evening exercise negatively affects sleep".
  - Exception: sleep-onset latency, total sleep time and sleep efficiency "might be impaired after vigorous exercise ending ≤1 h before bedtime".
  - Source: [Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0)
- **Frimpong et al. 2021 (Sleep Med Rev meta-analysis)**
  - 15 acute studies, 194 participants.
  - High-intensity exercise ending 0.5–4 h before bed reduced REM sleep by 2.34%. No other significant sleep changes were found.
  - Exercise 2–4 h before bed did not disrupt sleep in healthy adults aged 18–50. Regular evening high-intensity training also did not.
  - Source: [PMID 34416428](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34416428)
- **Leota et al. 2025 (Nature Communications)**
  - 14,689 active adults wearing WHOOP-type wearables, 4,084,354 person-nights. Observational.
  - Later and harder exercise was associated, dose-dependently, with later sleep onset, shorter and poorer sleep, higher nocturnal resting heart rate and lower HRV.
  - Exercise ending ≥4 h before sleep onset showed no association with sleep, whatever the strain.
  - Maximal vs light exercise ending 2 h before habitual sleep onset was linked to:
    - 36.0 min later sleep onset
    - 22.2 min (5.4%) shorter sleep
    - 6.8% higher resting heart rate
    - 14.1% lower HRV
  - Source: [Nat Commun / PMC12000559](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/)
  - The paper also notes that recent guidelines suggest moderate exercise ending ≥90 min before bed will not disrupt sleep — [same](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-58271-x).
- **Time-of-day performance (Knaier et al. 2022, MSSE meta-analysis)**
  - 63 studies; 29 were pooled.
  - Evening advantage, as effect sizes: endurance 0.23, Wingate peak power 0.73, handgrip 0.39, jump height 0.79.
  - "Strong evidence" that anaerobic power and jump height peak between 13:00 and 20:00. Handgrip strength peaks between 14:00 and 21:00. There is little evidence for a peak time in maximal endurance.
  - Source: [PMID 34431827](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34431827)
- **Training adaptation (Bruggisser et al. 2023, Sports Med Open)**
  - 26 studies were included.
  - No evidence that training at any particular time of day yields larger gains in performance or health. Gains are larger when training time matches testing time.
  - Source: [PMC10198889](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37208462)
- **Sleep loss and physical performance (Craven et al. 2022, Sports Med)**
  - 69 publications, 227 outcomes, 959 participants (89% male).
  - Sleep loss (≤6 h) reduced performance by −7.56% (95% CI −11.9 to −3.13).
  - Each extra hour awake before exercise reduced performance by about 0.4%.
  - Total deprivation and late restriction (waking early) were harmful. Morning tasks were largely unaffected; afternoon/evening tasks were consistently impaired.
  - The authors advise prioritising morning exercise when sleep loss can't be avoided.
  - Source: [PMID 35708888](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35708888)
- **Exercise for sleep inertia (Kovac et al. 2021)**
  - 15 participants were woken after a 2-h nocturnal nap (00:00–02:00).
  - A 30-s high-intensity sprint reduced subjective sleepiness and increased the cortisol awakening response. It did not improve cognitive performance or core body temperature.
  - Lesson: people may feel more alert than they actually are.
  - Source: [PMID 34606883](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34606883)
  - The Hilditch & McHill 2019 review had earlier classed exercise as a sleep-inertia countermeasure with "anecdotal support only" — [Nat Sci Sleep](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/).
- **DVT after air travel (Kuipers et al. 2007, PLoS Med)**
  - 8,755 employees, 38,910 person-years.
  - Venous thrombosis rate was 3.2 per 1,000 person-years in the 8 weeks after a ≥4-h flight, vs 1.0 unexposed (incidence rate ratio 3.2, 95% CI 1.8–5.6). That is about 1 event per 4,656 long-haul flights.
  - Risk was highest in the first 2 weeks. It rose with more and longer flights, and was higher in under-30s, women taking oral contraceptives, and very short, tall or overweight people.
  - Source: [PLoS Med](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17896862)

### Inferences
- **Hard workout rule:** end vigorous or maximal sessions ≥4 h before the planned sleep (conservative, based on Leota). If closer, suggest light or moderate activity, ending ≥90 min before sleep if possible. **Grade: moderate** (observational plus meta-analytic).
- **Hard training windows:** where the roster allows, put strength or power sessions in body-clock afternoon (about 13:00–20:00). **Grade: strong** for acute performance, **moderate/weak** for any long-term training advantage.
- **After a night flight or with sleep debt:**
  - Recommend recovery sleep first, then light activity only.
  - Discourage maximal or technical sessions, since performance drops about 7.6% with sleep loss and worsens with hours awake.
  - **Grade: moderate** for reduced performance. **Speculative** for injury risk (no direct data retrieved).
  - Before day sleep after a night duty, avoid vigorous exercise because it is close to sleep (extrapolated from evening-exercise data). **Grade: weak/speculative.**
- **After long-haul:** gentle walking or mobility is reasonable. The app should never discourage movement, and should list DVT warning signs (calf pain/swelling, breathlessness) and tell crew to seek care. **Grade: weak** for any protective effect of exercise; DVT risk itself is **strong**.
- Do not promise that exercise removes post-wake grogginess. It may only make crew *feel* more alert.

### Gaps
- No studies were found of exercise timed after a night shift and its effect on later day sleep in shift workers or crew.
- No data were retrieved on fatigue-related injury risk while exercising sleep-deprived, or on dehydration from cabin air and exercise.
- No cabin-crew-specific exercise trials were found.

## 3. Light: phase response curves, timing, and practical seek/avoid guidance

### Takeaway
Light is the strongest zeitgeber.
- Light before the core body temperature minimum (CBTmin, usually about 2–3 h before habitual wake) delays the clock. Light after CBTmin advances it.
- A single 6.7-h bright pulse shifts the clock by up to about 5 h peak-to-trough. A 1-h pulse gives about 40% of that.
- Even 10–50 lux in the evening measurably delays the clock.
- Well-run field protocols combine bright light at the right time, dark sunglasses at the wrong time, and a dark bedroom. They produced large delays in night workers.
- Evidence for blue-blocking glasses on sleep is weak.

**Grade: strong** for the PRC physiology. **Moderate** for combined light/dark protocols in shift work. **Weak/uncertain** for blue-blockers.

### Cited Findings
- **Khalsa et al. 2003**
  - 21 subjects. 6.7-h bright light (about 10,000 lux) under constant routine.
  - Produced a type 1 PRC with a 5.02-h peak-to-trough amplitude.
  - Light centred before CBTmin delayed the clock. Light after CBTmin advanced it. No shift occurred at CBTmin itself.
  - There was no prolonged "dead zone" during the subjective day.
  - Source: [J Physiol, PMID 12717008](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12717008)
- **St Hilaire et al. 2012**
  - 1-h pulse of about 8,000 lux. 18 bright-light and 18 dim-light subjects, at 18 different circadian phases.
  - Type 1 PRC with a 2.20-h amplitude, about 40% of the 6.7-h PRC despite only 15% of the exposure duration. This is a non-linear duration–response.
  - Source: [PubMed 22547633](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22547633)
- **Evening light sensitivity (Phillips et al. 2019, PNAS)**
  - Group ED50 for melatonin suppression was 24.6 lux.
  - 10, 30 and 50 lux in the evening delayed melatonin onset by 22, 77 and 109 min respectively.
  - Individual ED50 ranged from 6 to 350 lux.
  - Source: [PNAS, PMID 31138694](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31138694)
- **Consensus light targets (Brown et al. 2022, PLoS Biol)**
  - Daytime: at least 250 lux melanopic EDI at the eye.
  - Evening: at most 10 lux melanopic EDI, starting ≥3 h before bedtime.
  - Sleep environment: at most 1 lux melanopic EDI. If vision is needed at night, ≤10 lux.
  - Source: [PMC8929548](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35298459)
- **Bedroom light (Mason et al. 2022, PNAS)**: one night of sleeping in 100 lux light (vs <3 lux) raised nighttime heart rate, lowered HRV and increased insulin resistance the next morning in healthy adults — [PMID 35286195](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35286195).
- **Night-shift adaptation (Boivin & James 2002)**
  - Night-shift nurses: 10 in the treatment group, 9 controls.
  - Treatment: 6 h of intermittent workplace bright light (about 3,243 lux) plus tinted goggles (15% transmission) on the morning commute, with 8-h sleep in darkness starting 2 h after the shift.
  - Treatment delayed the temperature rhythm by 9.32 h and melatonin by 11.31 h, vs 4.09 h and 5.08 h in controls.
  - Source: [J Biol Rhythms, PMID 12465889](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12465889)
- **Night-shift adaptation (Crowley et al. 2003)**
  - 67 participants on 5 simulated night shifts.
  - Intermittent bright light (about 5,000 lux, 20 min on / 40 min off), dark sunglasses (2% vs 15% transmission) and a regular early daytime sleep (08:30–15:30) in a darkened room.
  - Bright light produced near-complete re-entrainment, and melatonin 1.8 mg SR added nothing on top.
  - With room light only, darker sunglasses helped.
  - The authors recommend intermittent bright light on shift, sunglasses "as dark as possible" on the commute home, and a regular early daytime dark/sleep period.
  - Source: [PMID 14667152](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14667152)
- **Compromise phase position (Smith, Fogg & Eastman 2009, SLEEP)**
  - Intermittent bright light on night shift, dark sunglasses outside, and scheduled sleep in darkness.
  - Partial re-entrainment (21 subjects) or complete re-entrainment (6) gave near-daytime levels of mood, fatigue and performance, vs 12 subjects who did not re-entrain.
  - Source: [PMID 19928387](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19928387)
- **Pre-flight advancing (Eastman et al. 2005, SLEEP)**
  - 26 participants. Sleep advanced 1 or 2 h/day for 3 days, plus intermittent morning bright light (about 5,000 lux).
  - DLMO advanced by a median of 1.4 h (1 h/day group) and 1.9 h (2 h/day group).
  - The 2 h/day schedule caused misalignment and trouble falling asleep. 1 h/day was recommended.
  - Source: [PMC1249488](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15700719)
- **Combined light and melatonin plans**: Revell & Eastman 2005 overlay the light and melatonin PRCs to time both, and argue jet lag could be prevented by shifting before the flight — [J Biol Rhythms, PMC3841977](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16077154). See also [Eastman & Burgess 2009, "How to travel the world without jet lag"](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20204161).
- **Cochrane: non-drug interventions for shift work (Slanger et al. 2016)**
  - 17 trials, 556 participants.
  - Bright light on night shift reduced Stanford Sleepiness Scale scores by 0.83 points (2 studies, 184 participants, very low-quality evidence).
  - Bright light plus sunglasses at dawn showed no significant effect on sleepiness (1 study, 17 participants).
  - Overall the evidence was inconclusive and low to very low quality.
  - Source: [CD010641](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27549931)
- **Cochrane: blue-light filtering lenses (Singh et al. 2023)**
  - 17 RCTs, 5–156 participants each.
  - Trials had high or unclear risk of bias. The abstract concludes evidence for any effect on sleep is uncertain.
  - The retrieved abstract was truncated before the sleep result; this conclusion follows the published review.
  - Source: [CD013244.pub2](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37593770)
- **Short layovers: stay on home time (Lowden & Åkerstedt 1998)**
  - 19 flight attendants on a 50-h westward layover (Copenhagen–Los Angeles, −9 h).
  - Keeping home-base sleep hours greatly reduced jet lag symptoms and sleepiness during the layover, compared with adopting local sleep hours.
  - Source: [Chronobiol Int, PMID 9706413](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9706413)

### Inferences
- **Core light rule for the app:**
  - Estimate CBTmin as about 2–3 h before habitual wake on the body clock.
  - "Seek light" after CBTmin to advance (eastward). "Seek light" before CBTmin to delay (westward or night-block).
  - "Avoid light / wear dark sunglasses" in the opposite window.
  - **Grade: strong** (lab PRCs). **Moderate** in the field.
  - Note: the CBTmin ≈ wake − 2–3 h rule of thumb comes from Eastman/Burgess's work but its numeric value was not re-extracted here.
- **Short layovers (≤ about 2 days) for crew:** default to staying on home time, with light/dark timed to home time. **Grade: moderate** (one crew field study, n=19, plus theory).
- **Night duties:**
  - Bright light in the first half of the night.
  - Dark sunglasses on the way home after a night duty.
  - Bedroom at ≤1 lux melanopic EDI (blackout curtains or eye mask).
  - **Grade: moderate.**
  - Caveat: full adaptation is rarely the goal for crew on rotating rosters. The "compromise" or partial approach is more realistic.
- **Evening light:** dim light to ≤10 lux melanopic EDI for 3 h before planned sleep. **Grade: moderate-strong.**
- **Blue-blocking glasses:** present as optional, low-evidence. Ordinary dark sunglasses outdoors have better support for "avoid light" windows. **Grade: weak.**

### Gaps
- No full text was available for Eastman & Burgess 2009 (Europe PMC returned nothing), so its exact light-box and timing recipes were not extracted.
- Light-box efficacy specifically in aircrew was not found.
- A Khalsa/St Hilaire PRC peak timing in hours relative to DLMO was not extracted.

## 4. Melatonin: PRC, dose, efficacy, safety, regulation, aviation policy

### Takeaway
- Melatonin works as a "darkness signal" whose PRC is roughly opposite to light's. Afternoon or early-evening doses advance the clock. Morning doses delay it. Bedtime doses have little phase effect but do help sleep.
- 0.5 mg and 3 mg give similar maximum shifts (about 1.5–1.8 h over 3 days) when each is timed optimally. The optimal time for 0.5 mg is later (2–4 h before DLMO) than for 3 mg (about 5 h before DLMO).
- Cochrane evidence supports melatonin for jet lag. Evidence for shift work is weak: about +24 min of day sleep.
- Short-term safety is good. Supplement quality is poor.
- Regulation varies widely by country. Aviation regulators require a waiting period: the FAA reportedly requires 24 h after the last dose before flying.

Because of all this, the app should treat melatonin as information-only, "discuss with your airline doctor / a physician".

**Grades:** PRC physiology **strong**. Jet lag benefit **moderate**. Shift work **weak**. Long-term safety **uncertain**.

### Cited Findings
- **Burgess, Revell & Eastman 2008 (J Physiol): 3 mg PRC**
  - 27 subjects.
  - Peak advance when taken about 5 h before DLMO (afternoon). Peak delay about 11 h after DLMO (soon after morning waking).
  - A dead zone sat around the first half of habitual sleep.
  - Fitted maximum shifts: +1.8 h advance and −1.3 h delay over 3 days.
  - Using melatonin as a nighttime sleep aid has minimal phase-shifting effects.
  - Source: [PMID 18006583](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/18006583)
- **Burgess et al. 2010 (JCEM): 0.5 mg PRC**
  - 34 adults (16 men, 18 women), aged 18–42.
  - Maximum advances occurred when 0.5 mg was taken in the afternoon, 2–4 h before DLMO (9–11 h before sleep midpoint). The best delay time peaked soon after waking.
  - The optimal time is later for the lower dose. When each dose is given at its optimal time, both yield similar advances and delays.
  - Source: [PMC2928909](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20410229)
- **Herxheimer & Petrie 2002 Cochrane review (jet lag)** — [CD001520](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001520/full). Key details from memory, **not re-verified in this session**:
  - 10 trials; melatonin reduced jet lag in 9.
  - Taken close to target bedtime (22:00–24:00) at the destination, for flights crossing ≥5 time zones.
  - 0.5–5 mg similarly effective; 5 mg gave faster sleep onset; slow-release 2 mg less effective.
  - The report writer should verify before quoting the numbers.
- **Liira et al. 2014 Cochrane review (shift work)**
  - 15 RCTs, 718 participants; 9 trials of melatonin.
  - Melatonin 1–10 mg after the night shift increased daytime sleep by about 24 min and night-time sleep by about 17 min vs placebo (low-quality evidence). It did not speed sleep onset.
  - Hypnotics (zopiclone) showed no association with outcomes. Modafinil and armodafinil improved alertness but caused headache and nausea.
  - Sources: [Cochrane summary](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD009776_drugs-treating-people-sleepiness-during-shift-work-and-sleep-problems-after-shift-work); [JAMA synopsis 2015](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25734738)
- **Carriedo-Diez et al. 2022 systematic review (health-care shift workers)**
  - 10 trials, doses 1–10 mg. Reported less sleepiness, shorter sleep latency and longer sleep.
  - Weaknesses: small samples and heterogeneous designs.
  - SIGN rating 1−, recommendation grade B.
  - Source: [IJERPH, PMC9408537](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9408537/)
- **Crowley et al. 2003**: melatonin 1.8 mg sustained-release before day sleep did not add to the phase delay produced by bright light and sunglasses — [PMID 14667152](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14667152).
- **Adverse events (Besag et al. 2019 systematic review)**
  - 37 RCTs, doses 0.15–12 mg, mostly ≤4 weeks.
  - Most common adverse events: daytime sleepiness 1.66%, headache 0.74%, dizziness 0.74%, hypothermia 0.62%. None were life-threatening.
  - Long-term safety data are scarce.
  - The review notes melatonin had "recently been licensed in the UK for the short-term treatment of jetlag".
  - Source: [CNS Drugs, PMID 31722088](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/31722088)
- **Product quality: Erland & Saxena 2017**
  - 31 supplements tested.
  - Melatonin content ranged from −83% to +478% of the label. Lot-to-lot variation reached 465%.
  - More than 71% were outside ±10% of the label claim. 26% contained serotonin.
  - Source: [JCSM, PMC5263083](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27855744)
- **Product quality: Cohen et al. 2023 (JAMA)**: tested melatonin and CBD content in US melatonin gummies. Specific numbers were not extracted (research letter, no abstract) — [PMID 37097362](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/37097362).
- **Child ingestions (Lelak et al. 2022, MMWR)**
  - 260,435 paediatric melatonin ingestions in the US from 2012 to 2021, a 530% increase.
  - 5 children needed mechanical ventilation and 2 died.
  - Source: [MMWR](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35653284)
- **US regulatory status**: regulated by the FDA as a dietary supplement and sold over the counter — [Lelak 2022 MMWR](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35653284).
- **India regulatory status** (sources conflict or are weak):
  - A consumer blog states melatonin is listed in FSSAI's health-supplement/nutraceutical schedule (2–10 mg/day) and sold over the counter — [unboxhealth blog, low reliability](https://blog.unboxhealth.in/melatonin-side-effects/).
  - Business Standard (Feb 2024) reports a government panel reviewing whether nutraceuticals should move under CDSCO. Officials noted companies shifting "drug-like" ingredients such as melatonin from CDSCO to FSSAI approval — [Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/health/govt-panel-set-to-review-if-nutraceuticals-should-be-brought-under-cdsco-124021800226_1.html).
  - Melatonin is also sold in India as a prescription-type tablet product ([example manufacturer listing](https://www.neuraclelife.com/product/melonap-3/)).
  - **Status: grey zone, not verified from FSSAI/CDSCO primary text.**
- **FAA (pilots)**
  - A search summary of FAA AME-guide material reported a 24-h wait after the last melatonin dose. The general rule for sleep aids is a wait of 5× the maximum elimination half-life.
  - Not verified on the page itself (403 error) — [FAA AME Guide: sleep aids](https://www.faa.gov/ame_guide/pharm/sleepaids); [FAA OTC medications PDF](https://www.faa.gov/pilots/medical_certification/media/OTCMedicationsforPilots.pdf).
- **AsMA position paper (Caldwell et al. 2009, Aviat Space Environ Med)** reviews pre-flight, post-flight and in-flight fatigue countermeasures, including drugs. Its specific melatonin position statement was not extracted — [PMID 19180856](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19180856).

### Inferences
- If the app mentions melatonin at all, use PRC-based timing:
  - **To advance (eastward):** 0.5 mg about 2–4 h before body-clock DLMO, or 3 mg about 5 h before (afternoon or early evening, body-clock time).
  - **To delay:** morning dosing, which also causes sleepiness, so it is impractical before duty.
  - **As a sleep aid:** at target bedtime (the Cochrane jet-lag approach).
  - **Low dose (0.5 mg)** is preferable: equal shift and likely less hangover. Grade **moderate**; the hangover point is an inference.
- **Mistimed melatonin can shift the clock the wrong way.** A daytime dose before duty risks sleepiness on duty. The app must never schedule melatonin within the aviation waiting period (use a ≥24-h no-duty buffer as a conservative default until the airline's or regulator's rule is confirmed). **Grade: strong (safety principle).**
- **Responsible framing:**
  - Information only, not a dosing instruction.
  - "Check legality in your destination and your airline medical policy; first try on a day off; avoid if pregnant, on anticoagulants or epilepsy medication, etc."
  - Advise pharmaceutical-grade products, given 71% label inaccuracy in supplements.
  - Keep it opt-in, as the current app already does.
- **Shift-work melatonin:** expect a modest effect (about +24 min of day sleep). Don't oversell.

### Gaps (unverified due to search budget — important for product/legal review)
- India: exact FSSAI schedule entry, dose limits, and CDSCO prescription status as of 2026.
- UAE (MOHAP/EDE) and Qatar (MoPH): prescription/OTC status and any rules for carrying it in luggage. Not found.
- UK: prescription-only (POM) status and the licensed jet-lag product (only the indirect Besag 2019 mention was found).
- EU: EFSA-authorised claims (0.5 mg for jet lag, 1 mg for sleep onset, under Reg. 432/2012) and national dose limits. Not verified.
- EASA, UK CAA, ICAO Doc 8984 and DGCA guidance on melatonin use by aircrew (waiting periods, ground trial): not retrieved. Cabin crew are often not covered by pilot medical rules. Airline medical policy is likely the binding rule.
- Herxheimer 2002 figures need verification. Any 2020–2026 update or replacement Cochrane jet-lag review was not checked.
- The reported 2025 observational signal linking long-term melatonin use to heart failure was not retrieved or verified.

## 5. Naps: duration, sleep inertia, timing, prophylactic and caffeine-naps

### Takeaway
- Short naps of about 10–20 min give the fastest benefit with minimal grogginess.
- Naps of 30 min or more, or naps near the circadian low, carry sleep inertia. It typically fades within 15–30 min but can last ≥1 h.
- A prophylactic nap before a night of sleep loss beats a nap taken later.
- Caffeine taken just before a short nap gives the best combined effect.
- The current app's "26-minute NASA nap" refers to sleep *obtained* in a 40-min opportunity, not a target duration.

**Grades:** short-nap benefit **strong** (lab). Prophylactic nap **moderate**. Caffeine-nap **moderate**. On-shift naps improving sleepiness **moderate**, improving performance **weak**.

### Cited Findings
- **Brooks & Lack 2006 (SLEEP)**
  - 24 adults after about 5 h of night sleep, nap at 15:00.
  - 5-min nap: little benefit.
  - 10-min nap: immediate improvements lasting up to 155 min.
  - 20-min nap: benefits appeared at 35 min and lasted to 125 min.
  - 30-min nap: initial impairment (sleep inertia), then benefits up to 155 min.
  - Source: [PMID 16796222](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16796222)
- **Tietzel & Lack 2001**
  - 12 adults after 4.7 h of sleep.
  - A 10-min nap improved alertness and performance immediately for 1 h. A 30-min nap at first worsened them.
  - Source: [PMID 11322712](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/11322712)
- **Hilditch, Dorrian & Banks 2017 review (naps ≤30 min)**
  - There are very few studies of short *night* naps, with mixed results on slow-wave sleep and sleep inertia.
  - Short naps usually avoid inertia because slow-wave sleep typically starts after about 30 min.
  - Source: [Sleep Med 32:176-190](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1389945717300023)
- **Hilditch & McHill 2019 (Nat Sci Sleep): sleep inertia**
  - Often resolves within about 30 min, but full recovery may take ≥1 h. Some effects lasted up to 3.5 h.
  - Impairment right after waking can equal or exceed that of 24 h of sleep deprivation.
  - It is worse at the circadian low, after prior sleep loss, and after slow-wave sleep or long naps.
  - Pre-nap caffeine is the most effective countermeasure. Light, noise and face-washing have limited evidence.
  - Recommends avoiding safety-critical tasks in the first 30 min and allowing up to 1 h buffer.
  - Source: [PMC6710480](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)
- **Dinges et al. 1987 (prophylactic naps)**
  - 41 adults, each given a 2-h nap at one of five points in a 56-h period without other sleep.
  - Early naps (after 6–18 h awake) gave larger and longer-lasting performance benefits, lasting >24 h. Circadian peak vs trough placement didn't matter.
  - "Napping prior to a night of sleep loss is more important" than circadian placement. Subjects were unaware of the benefit.
  - Source: [PMID 3659730](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/3659730)
- **Schweitzer et al. 2006**
  - Lab study: 68 people. Field study: 53 night or rotating shift workers.
  - An evening nap before night shifts plus caffeine gave the best alertness and performance. Both components helped on their own.
  - Source: [PMID 16453980](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/16453980)
- **Caffeine plus nap (Reyner & Horne 1997)**
  - 12 sleepy drivers: 200 mg caffeine plus a <15-min nap.
  - Driving incidents fell to 9% of placebo levels, vs 34% with caffeine alone. Even "non-sleep dozing" helped.
  - Source: [PMID 9401427](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/9401427)
- **Caffeine plus nap (Hayashi et al. 2003)**
  - 10 adults, 20-min nap at 12:40.
  - Caffeine (200 mg) plus nap was best for sleepiness and performance for 1 h. Bright light after the nap was comparable except on performance. Face-washing helped briefly.
  - Source: [PMID 14652086](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14652086)
- **Caffeine-nap at night (Centofanti et al. 2020 pilot)**
  - Only 6 participants: 200 mg caffeine before a 30-min nap at 03:30.
  - Improved vigilance and fatigue in the 45 min after the nap.
  - Source: [PMID 32819191](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32819191)
- **On-shift naps (Purnell et al. 2002)**
  - 24 aircraft maintenance engineers on 12-h night shifts, 20-min nap between 01:00 and 03:00.
  - Improved vigilance at the end of the first night shift only. No effect on drive-home sleepiness or later sleep.
  - Source: [PMID 12220318](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12220318)
- **On-shift naps (Martin-Gill et al. 2018 meta-analysis, EMS and similar shift workers)**
  - 13 studies.
  - Naps significantly reduced end-of-shift sleepiness (SMD 0.40, 95% CI 0.09–0.72). Effect on reaction time was not significant (SMD 0.12).
  - Source: [PMID 29324083](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29324083)
- **On-shift naps (Ruggiero & Redeker 2014 review)**: 13 small studies. Night naps reduced sleepiness and performance deficits despite brief sleep inertia. No safety-outcome data — [PMC4079545](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23411360).
- **Cochrane (Slanger et al. 2016)**: 4 of its 17 trials studied napping opportunities. Overall evidence low or very low — [CD010641](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27549931).
- **In-flight sleep (Signal et al. 2013)**
  - 21 pilots, polysomnography during a 7-h in-flight rest on flights averaging 15.7 h.
  - Sleep efficiency was 70% vs 88% in the hotel, with 7.7 vs 4.6 awakenings per hour and almost no slow-wave sleep (median 0.5%).
  - Source: [PMC3524531](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23288977)
- **NASA planned cockpit rest (Rosekind et al. 1994, NASA TM-108839)**: commonly cited as a 40-min rest opportunity with about 26 min (25.8) of actual sleep and improved vigilance afterwards. **Not verified this session.**

### Inferences
- **App default nap guidance:**
  - "Power nap": 10–20 min of sleep (set an alarm about 20–25 min after lying down to allow time to fall asleep). **Grade: strong.**
  - Relabel "26-min NASA nap" as "about 25 min nap, as in the NASA cockpit-rest study (40-min opportunity)". Warn that grogginess is likely if it runs past about 30 min or falls in the early-morning circadian low. **Grade: moderate.**
  - Longer naps (about 90 min, a full cycle) only when there is ≥30–60 min before duty or driving. This 90-min rationale is inference; no study retrieved here.
- **Before a night or late duty:** recommend a prophylactic nap in the afternoon or evening. **Grade: moderate.**
- **Caffeine-nap:** caffeine (about 200 mg in the studies) immediately before a ≤20-min nap. Only when it won't hurt the next main sleep (that timing is outside this scope; coordinate with the caffeine module). **Grade: moderate.**
- After any nap, suggest light, cold water on the face, and 15–30 min before safety-critical tasks; up to 60 min after long or nighttime naps. **Grade: moderate.**
- In-flight crew-rest sleep is lighter and less restorative than sleep on the ground. Don't count it 1:1 toward sleep need. **Grade: moderate.**

### Gaps
- Rosekind 1994 figures (sleep latency, PVT improvement percentages) were not retrieved from primary NTRS.
- No cabin-crew-specific nap trials were retrieved.
- 90-min nap evidence was not retrieved.

## 6. Sleep scheduling: anchor sleep, split sleep, minimum sleep, early starts, recovery

### Takeaway
- Keeping about 4 h of "anchor sleep" at a fixed time each day stabilises circadian rhythms despite irregular other sleep.
- Performance depends mostly on total sleep per 24 h, so split sleep (core sleep plus a nap) works when the total is adequate.
- Chronic sleep of 6 h or less causes cumulative deficits that people don't notice.
- Each hour a duty starts earlier than 09:00 costs about 15 min of sleep.
- One night of recovery sleep is not enough after multiple restricted nights.

**Grade: moderate–strong** (lab dose-response studies), anchor sleep **moderate** (small, old isolation studies).

### Cited Findings
- **Minors & Waterhouse 1981**
  - Isolation-unit volunteers.
  - Irregular sleep (random 8 h, or two random 4-h blocks) led to free-running rhythms longer than 24 h.
  - When one 4-h "anchor" block was kept at a fixed time each day (tested at 00–04, 04–08, 08–12 or 12–16), rhythms re-stabilised to 24 h within a few days. Sleep timing, not mealtimes, was the synchroniser.
  - Source: [PMID 7239725](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/7239725)
- **Minors & Waterhouse 1983**: constant-routine data confirmed an endogenous component stabilised to 24 h by anchor sleep — [J Physiol, PMID 6663508](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/6663508).
- **Mollicone et al. 2008**
  - 90 subjects on 18 split-sleep regimens (night anchor plus daytime nap).
  - PVT performance and sleepiness depended mainly on total time in bed per 24 h, however it was divided.
  - Source: [PMC2633932](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/19194521)
- **Jackson, Banks & Belenky 2014**
  - 53 men.
  - Split 5 h + 5 h (03:00–08:00 and 15:00–20:00) gave 7.16 h of sleep, vs 8.4 h for 10-h night sleep and 6.4 h for 10-h day sleep.
  - Daytime consolidated sleep was shortest and gave the most sleepiness. Performance did not differ.
  - Source: [PMID 25222346](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25222346)
- **Lo et al. 2020 (adolescents)**
  - With 8 h total, split and continuous sleep performed equally.
  - With 6.5 h total, splitting with an afternoon nap was better than continuous sleep.
  - Source: [SLEEP, PMID 32619240](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/32619240)
- **Van Dongen et al. 2003**
  - 48 adults, 14 days of 4 h, 6 h or 8 h in bed.
  - 6 h or less produced deficits equal to up to 2 nights of total sleep deprivation, while subjective sleepiness plateaued (people were unaware of the decline).
  - Lapses rose with wakefulness beyond about 15.84 h per day.
  - Source: [PMID 12683469](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12683469)
- **Sleep need**: the AASM/SRS consensus is ≥7 h per night for adults — [Watson et al. 2015, JCSM](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25979105). The abstract retrieved did not show the number; the ≥7 h figure is from the published statement.
- **Recovery sleep (Banks et al. 2010)**
  - 159 adults: 5 nights at 4 h in bed, then one recovery night of 0–10 h in bed.
  - Recovery was incomplete even after 10 h in bed (8.96 h of sleep) for PVT, sleepiness and fatigue. Multiple nights may be needed.
  - Source: [PMC2910531](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/20815182)
- **Early starts (Roach et al. 2012)**
  - 70 short-haul pilots.
  - Sleep in the 12 h before duty was 5.4 h for 04:00–05:00 starts vs 6.6 h for 09:00–10:00 starts. That is about 15 min lost per hour of earlier start before 09:00.
  - Fatigue at duty start was highest for the earliest starts.
  - Source: [PMID 22239926](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/22239926)
- **Early starts vs late finishes (Arsintescu et al. 2022)**
  - 95 pilots.
  - Sleep before early starts was 6.94 h vs 8.47 h before late finishes.
  - Early starts gave worse pre-duty fatigue. Late finishes gave worse post-duty fatigue.
  - Source: [PMC9286802](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34854507)
- **Night shift and day sleep (Åkerstedt 2003 review)**: night shifts cause difficulty falling asleep, shortened day sleep and sleepiness that carries into days off — [Occup Med, PMID 12637592](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/12637592).
- **Day sleep is short (Jackson 2014)**: daytime sleep (10:00–20:00 in bed) gave only 6.4 h of sleep — [PMID 25222346](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/25222346).
- **Light/dark plan for day sleep (Crowley 2003)**: sleeping at a regular early time after night shift (08:30–15:30, darkened room) combined with the light/dark plan allowed full adaptation — [PMID 14667152](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/14667152).

### Inferences
- **Anchor sleep:** recommend a protected 4-h anchor block at a consistent home-time slot across irregular rosters. Fill the rest with naps or split sleep. **Grade: moderate.**
- **Daily sleep goal:** set a 24-h target of ≥7 h of sleep (summing main sleep and naps). Flag when the projected total is ≤6 h, or when continuous wakefulness will exceed about 16 h before or during duty. **Grade: strong.**
- **Early starts:**
  - Advise going to bed earlier, but expect the circadian "wake maintenance zone" to limit how early sleep can come.
  - Add a nap after the duty.
  - Budget about 15 min less sleep per hour of start before 09:00.
  - **Grade: moderate.** The wake-maintenance point is from general knowledge, not retrieved here.
- **After a night duty:**
  - Go to bed soon after duty in darkness, but expect a shortened sleep.
  - Top up with an afternoon nap and a normal-time sleep that night (split recovery).
  - Plan ≥2 nights of recovery after consecutive short-sleep duties.
  - **Grade: moderate** for the components. The exact "short morning sleep + early night" recipe is **weak** (no trial retrieved).

### Gaps
- No cabin-crew sleep datasets were retrieved for IndiGo, Air India or Gulf carriers.
- No direct trial comparing post-night-duty recovery strategies (short morning sleep vs full day sleep) was retrieved.
- AASM/SRS numeric recommendation and Rosekind 1994 were not verified from primary text this session.
