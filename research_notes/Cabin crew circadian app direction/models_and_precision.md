## 1. Which validated models exist, what they predict, what inputs they need, and how well they are validated

### Takeaway
All the established fatigue models (SAFTE/FAST, Three-Process/BAM, FAID, CAS, Interactive Neurobehavioral/Kronauer) come from the same two-process core: sleep pressure plus a circadian rhythm. In blind tests they predict **group-average** fatigue reasonably well after acute sleep loss. They predict poorly under chronic sleep restriction, and they do **not** predict individuals. The best aviation evidence for cabin crew (SAFTE-FAST, 178 US flight attendants) shows strong agreement only once performance is **averaged across bins of predictions**. Light-driven oscillator models (Kronauer/Forger/St Hilaire/Hannay) predict circadian phase (DLMO), not fatigue. They are the right tool for timing light advice, but they need light or activity data, not just a roster.

### Cited Findings
**Model families and the 2002 blind comparison**
- At the 2002 Fatigue and Performance Modeling Workshop, six models were given sleep/wake scenarios without seeing the data: CHS Chronic Fatigue Model (Spencer & Belyavin), Circadian Alertness Simulator (Moore-Ede), FAID (Dawson & Fletcher), Interactive Neurobehavioral Model (Jewett & Kronauer), SAFTE (Hursh) and Sleep/Wake Predictor/Three-Process Model (Folkard & Åkerstedt). — [Van Dongen 2004, Aviat Space Environ Med 75(3 Suppl):A15–36](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Van_Dongen_ASEM_75_3_2004.pdf)
- The models predicted some scenarios "fairly well" (88 h of extended wakefulness with or without naps; a field study of locomotive engineers). However, "none of the models predicted these chronic sleep restriction scenarios well." For one model, the predictive power for PVT under chronic restriction was "almost the same as that of a horizontal line." — [Van Dongen 2004](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Van_Dongen_ASEM_75_3_2004.pdf)
- "Differences among the predictions from the six models were relatively small, suggesting that these models have a broad common basis… not one model clearly stood out as the overall best or worst." Comparisons were made only after linear scaling of each model's output to the data (mixed-effects regression), so the raw model units were never directly validated. — [Van Dongen 2004](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Van_Dongen_ASEM_75_3_2004.pdf)
- The models "all tended to overestimate subjective sleepiness and performance impairment at the end of the day." The paper also notes that in transport, sleep times "are often irregular and unknown — but work times are usually logged." FAID ran from work times only. — [Van Dongen 2004](https://www.med.upenn.edu/uep/assets/user-content/documents/Van_Dongen_ASEM_75_3_2004.pdf)

**SAFTE / FAST (Hursh; IBR; US Army/Air Force origin)**
- Inputs: sleep/wake/work history. Where sleep is unknown, a proprietary "Auto-Sleep" algorithm imputes it from the work schedule. Output: "Effectiveness", defined as PVT speed (1/RT) as a % of optimum baseline. The model was originally developed for the US Army and Air Force. — [FAA DOT/FAA/AM-12/12 (Roma, Hursh, Mead, Nesthus 2012)](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf); [Wikipedia: FAST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatigue_Avoidance_Scheduling_Tool)
- **Rail accident validation (retrospective):** the model was run on 30-day work histories before 400 human-factors and 1,000 non-human-factors freight-rail accidents. It "had no predictive power for non-human factors accidents." For human-factors accidents, relative risk rose as effectiveness fell, starting at 90% and increasing linearly. Relative economic risk was about 5× higher when effectiveness was at or below 77% and about 75% lower when it was above 90% (Hursh, Fanzone & Raslear 2011). — [FAA AM-12/12 background section](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf); [FRA fatigue white paper](https://railroads.dot.gov/sites/fra.dot.gov/files/fra_net/2944/fatiguewhitepaper112706.pdf)
- More than 1 million 30-minute work intervals covering more than 57,000 work starts were evaluated. "Effectiveness scores below 70 were associated with a reliable increase in human factors accident risk." "An effectiveness score of 70 is the rough equivalent of a 0.08 blood alcohol level or being awake for 21 hours" (FRA materials). — [search summary of FRA reports](https://railroads.dot.gov/sites/fra.dot.gov/files/fra_net/2944/fatiguewhitepaper112706.pdf); [Validation and Calibration of a Fatigue Assessment Tool for Railroad Work Schedules (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234082087_Validation_and_Calibration_of_a_Fatigue_Assessment_Tool_for_Railroad_Work_Schedules_Final_Report)
- **Cabin-crew field validation (most relevant to CrewCircadian):** 178 US flight attendants (from 202 completers; network, low-cost and regional carriers; domestic and international) were followed for 3–4 weeks each. Sleep was measured by actigraphy (ReadiBand) and alertness by 10,659 valid 5-minute PVTs. Linear regression of **mean** PVT performance across **5% SAFTE prediction bins** gave R² = 0.884 for Actual Effectiveness, 0.745 for RT and 0.486 for lapses. For trip-only sessions (n = 7,533) R² was 0.889, and for pre-work sessions (n = 1,712) R² was 0.530. — [Roma et al. 2012, DOT/FAA/AM-12/12](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf)
- The same report found "high variability in mean PVT performances… particularly at and below the 75% bin." It attributes this to individual differences in sleep need and vulnerability: "not every individual will exhibit performance impairments… under sleep/wake/work patterns expected to produce fatigue." It also states: "Most model predictions represent a population average, and the SAFTE model is no exception," and notes that developing a priori individual-level models for "very large and exceptionally mobile populations such as commercial aviation" remains unsolved. It suggests the dataset could be used to "validate and/or calibrate the current Auto-Sleep function specifically for commercial aviation", which implies Auto-Sleep had not been aviation-calibrated at that time. — [Roma et al. 2012](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf)

**Three-Process Model of Alertness (Åkerstedt & Folkard), basis of Jeppesen/Boeing tooling**
- Ingre et al. 2014 drew on 136 aircrew (captains, first officers, cabin crew; crowd-sourced through a Jeppesen website, 2011–12), with 964 sleep logs, 5,443 sleeps and KSS ratings. The fitted model (S + C + ultradian U, plus a "brake" function) had a residual error SD of **1.42 KSS units** (on a 1–9 scale) and a between-subject intercept SD of 0.84. Replacing observed sleep with model-generated sleep raised the residual to 1.46. — [Ingre et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9(10):e108679](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)
- Adjusting circadian phase using a single chronotype question shifted predicted phase across about 2 h (14.61 h for morning types to 16.62 h for extreme evening types). The best jet-lag acclimatisation rate was about **30% per day**, not the previously assumed 50%. The model was extended to output the probability of KSS ≥ 7, with reference limits for 50/75/90% of individuals as well as the group mean. — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)
- Stated limitations: self-selected, crowd-sourced participants ("no guarantee that participating individuals were who they claimed to be"), not representative of all operations, and "individual differences are likely to play a role." — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)

**Physiologically based sleep/arousal model (Phillips–Robinson lineage; Postnova "Model of Arousal Dynamics")**
- Knock, Postnova et al. 2021 studied 21 ICU nurses on rotating day, evening and night shifts for 2–3 weeks, using **group-average default parameters** (no individual calibration). Inputs tested were the shift schedule alone, plus commute times, plus actigraphy light. Sleep/wake was predicted with 81–87% minute-by-minute overlap (73% sleep overlap from the shift schedule alone). KSS mean absolute error was 1.45–1.55 on day shifts and 1.50–1.66 on night shifts; 56–60% of KSS values were within ±1 by day and 48–52% at night. Circadian phase MAE was 0.87–1.42 h; 65% of nurses were within ±1 h on day schedules but only 35–47% on night schedules. The model has no sleep inertia and does not predict pre-shift naps, which about 30% of participants took. — [Knock et al. 2021, SLEEP 44(11):zsab146](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/11/zsab146/6295924)

**Light-driven circadian oscillators (Kronauer → Jewett 1999 → Forger 1999 "simpler" → St Hilaire 2007 non-photic → Hannay 2019 macroscopic)**
- These predict circadian phase (e.g., DLMO) from light (or activity as a proxy). In Huang et al. 2021, "all four models performed similarly when given identical inputs." — [Huang et al. 2021, SLEEP 44(10):zsab126](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/10/zsab126/6278480)
- Accuracy figures are given in Section 2. Stone et al. 2019: in 25 rotating night-shift nurses and doctors, MAE was 0.69 h on day schedules (80% within ±1 h) and 0.95 h on night shifts (68% within ±1 h). The phase *shift* was within ±1 h for only 56% of individuals. — [Stone et al. 2019, Sci Rep](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6667480/)

**Individualised performance model (US Army Unified Model / 2B-Alert)**
- 2B-Alert adds Bayesian learning to the Unified Model of Performance and adapts to an individual after each in-app PVT. In a prospective 62-hour total sleep deprivation study (n = 21), fit RMSE was 24 ms during learning and prediction RMSE was 50 ms. After about 12 PVTs, accuracy approached the peak achieved with all tests. — [Reifman et al. 2019, J Sleep Res e12725](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.12725)
- 2B-Alert App 2.0 adds personalised caffeine recommendations. — [Vital-Lopez et al. 2023, SLEEP 46(7):zsad080](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/46/7/zsad080/7092866)

**Regulator and industry position on what models are for**
- CASA (Australia): models "tend to be based on averaged fatigue ratings and are restricted to predicting risk probabilities for a target population average rather than instantaneous fatigue levels of a specific individual." Limitations also include "qualitative data being misinterpreted as quantitative data and limited validation against aviation specific data." Model predictions "should never form the sole means" of operational decisions. — [CASA Biomathematical Fatigue Models Guidance](https://www.casa.gov.au/biomathematical-fatigue-models-guidance); [IATA condensed CASA summary](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/condensed-version-of-casa-biomathematical-models-doc.pdf)
- IATA Human Factors Task Force white paper (April 2025): "Model outputs represent the population average and may not be accurate for specific individuals"; "Model sleep predictions may not reflect actual sleep duration and quality which are fundamental to the validity of a model's output"; "The output of a model does not necessarily correlate to a safety risk"; models "should not be used by individuals, operators or regulators as the sole go/no-go decision-making tool"; models "may be better at detecting fatigue risk related to sleep restriction, instead of… workload and long duties." — [IATA HFTF 2025 white paper](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/iata_hftf_bmm_limitations_of_fatigue_model_guidance_april_2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- The FAA flight-attendant R² values of 0.88 are **binned-mean** correlations. They show that the model ranks conditions correctly on average. They are **not** evidence that a single crew member's score at a given hour is accurate, and individual-session agreement is not reported in the abstract. The product should not quote "88% accurate".
- Given an error SD of about 1.4 KSS points on a 9-point scale (Ingre; Knock), a validated model cannot reliably tell a KSS of 5 from 7 for one person at one hour.
- Accident-risk validation exists only for **US freight rail** (retrospective). No published accident or incident validation exists for cabin crew. Aviation validation is against PVT and KSS.
- Every model degrades when sleep is imputed rather than measured. A roster-only app is in the "Auto-Sleep / model-generated sleep" regime, the least accurate one.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved this session, but known in the literature: Borbély 1982 and Daan/Beersma/Borbély 1984 original two-process papers; Borbély et al. 2016 "reappraisal" (J Sleep Res); Åkerstedt & Folkard 1997 and Åkerstedt et al. 2004 three-process predictions; Hursh et al. 2004 (SAFTE); Roach/Fletcher/Dawson 2004 (FAID); Moore-Ede et al. 2004 (CAS trucking); Dawson et al. 2011 "Modelling fatigue and the use of fatigue models in work settings" (Accid Anal Prev); Van Dongen et al. 2004 on trait-like vulnerability; McCauley et al. 2013 and Rajdev et al. 2013 chronic-restriction models; Postnova et al. 2018. My attempt to verify these via Europe PMC failed with an SSL error. Exact figures from them are not included.
- Published validation error ranges for **FAID** and **Jeppesen BAM / Crew Alertness** specifically, and the FRA Auto-Sleep vs actigraphy validation, were not retrieved.
- No source found on how "Jeppesen BAM/CAS" relates to Moore-Ede's Circadian Alertness Simulator (CAS). These are different products; the naming should be checked before citing.

## 2. How accurately can an individual's circadian phase be estimated without lab measurement?

### Takeaway
For people with **regular day schedules**, models fed by wearable activity or light estimate DLMO with a mean absolute error of about **0.6–1.0 h**, about 70–80% within ±1 h. Sleep timing alone gives about 1 h MAE. For **shift workers, the population most like cabin crew**, accuracy collapses. One study found 2.5–3.8 h MAE with only 15–26% within ±1 h. Using sleep times as a proxy gave 3.9–6.5 h MAE. Chronotype questionnaires leave about a **4 h range** of true DLMO at any given score. Light sensitivity varies more than 50-fold between people, and intrinsic period varies by ±0.2 h (SD). A roster-only app therefore cannot know an individual crew member's body-clock time to better than several hours once they are rotating through early, late and overnight duties.

### Cited Findings
- **Huang et al. 2021 (SLEEP), comparison of models and wearables against measured DLMO:**
  - Day workers (n = 10): the best model with activity input had MAE 0.61 h (80% within 1 h, 100% within 2 h). Light inputs gave MAE 0.83–1.49 h. A "sleep timing proxy" gave MAE 0.97 h. Fitting a cosinor to activity gave MAE 3.05 h (SD 2.45 h) and "does not appropriately reflect circadian phase."
  - Apple Watch cohort (test n = 15): MAE 0.96 h, 73% within 1 h. Sleep-timing proxy: 1.02 h.
  - **Shift workers (n = 27): model MAE 2.5–2.8 h with activity input (18.5–25.9% within 1 h; 44–56% within 2 h) and 3.6–3.8 h with light input. Using post-shift sleep timing as a proxy gave MAE 6.54 h (11% within 3 h); non-work-day sleep timing gave 3.86 h (29.6% within 2 h).**
  - Activity was better than wrist-measured light in shift workers (p < 0.0001). The authors' conclusion: "In those living under normal living conditions, circadian phase can typically be predicted to within 1 h."
  - — [Huang et al. 2021, SLEEP zsab126](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/10/zsab126/6278480)
- Stone et al. 2019 (25 rotating night-shift healthcare staff; wrist light and activity into a limit-cycle model; urinary aMT6s reference): MAE 0.69 h on day schedules (80% within ±1 h) and 0.95 h after 3–5 nights (68% within ±1 h). The phase shift was within ±1 h for only 56% of individuals: "prediction of inter-individual variability in circadian phase shift during night shifts was limited." — [Stone et al. 2019, Sci Rep](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6667480/)
- Knock et al. 2021 (21 ICU nurses; group-average model): phase within ±1 h in 65% (day) vs 35–47% (night); MAE 0.87–1.42 h. — [Knock et al. 2021, SLEEP](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/11/zsab146/6295924)
- Weed et al. 2025 (J Biol Rhythms; particle-filter St Hilaire 2007 model, light-only input; 56 regular-schedule and 32 shift workers): Lin's concordance was 0.93 (regular) vs 0.89 (shift) with 14-day recordings. Longer recordings helped regular-schedule participants but "did not exhibit consistent improvements" in shift workers. Each +100 lux in mean illuminance cut RMSE by 0.75 h. "Shift workers exhibited less bright and more irregular day-to-day light exposure patterns and showed minimal accuracy gains despite longer recordings." — [Weed et al. 2025, PMC12694990](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12694990/)
- **Questionnaires and chronotype:** in Kantermann, Sung & Burgess 2015 (N = 60, ages 18–62, 24 of them with delayed sleep phase disorder), DLMO correlated with MEQ (r = −0.70) and MCTQ MSFsc (r = 0.68). MEQ + MSFsc + age explained 60% of DLMO variance. Even so, "around a 4-h range in the DLMO was observed at a given MEQ score and a given MSFsc, indicating that neither questionnaire should be exclusively used to time light or exogenous melatonin treatment, as this could result in the mistiming of these treatments… thereby potentially worsening circadian misalignment." Melatonin typically rises 2–3 h before usual sleep onset. — [Kantermann et al. 2015, J Biol Rhythms 30(5):449–453](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58750/1/Kantermann_Comparing_the_morningness-eveningness_questionnaire.pdf)
- **Population spread of DLMO:** a meta-analysis/pooled individual-data review (3,579 individuals from 121 studies; individual data on 1,749) found mean salivary DLMO of 21:39 ± 1.56 h (SD) at ages 20–29, 21:27 ± 1.46 h at 30–39 and 21:11 ± 1.37 h at 40–49. There was no sex effect, and higher morningness was associated with earlier DLMO (p < 0.0001). — [DLMO across ages, SLEEP 2023, PMC10171641](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10171641/)
- **Intrinsic period (tau):** 24.15 ± 0.2 h (n = 157, month-long inpatient forced desynchrony). Women 24.09 h vs men 24.19 h; 35% of women vs 14% of men had tau < 24.0 h. — [Duffy et al. 2011, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1010666108)
- **Light sensitivity:** across 55 people, 50% melatonin suppression occurred at < 30 lux, with "a >50-fold difference in sensitivity to evening light across individuals." A low-sensitivity person at 400 lux responded like a high-sensitivity person at 10 lux. — [Phillips et al. 2019, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1901824116)
- Light sensitivity and intrinsic period have been examined specifically as determinants of individual circadian timing predictions (title only retrieved). — [Stone et al. 2020, J Biol Rhythms](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0748730420962598)
- **Adaptation in shift work:** a review of 6 melatonin studies found < 3% of permanent night workers fully adjust and < 1 in 4 adjust "substantially" enough to benefit. — [Folkard 2008, Chronobiol Int 25:215–224, via Wikidata record](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q37183271) [search summary]
- **Aircrew on short layovers:** 39 pilots flying multiple transpacific trips with 1–2-day layovers were measured by actigraphy, PVT and Samn-Perelli. Circadian rhythm stayed aligned to home-base time before and after the trip, while layover sleep shifted toward local night, creating misalignment. — [Gander et al. 2016, Chronobiol Int 33(8)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27253676/) [abstract summary]
- Jet-lag acclimatisation in aircrew KSS data fitted best at about 30% of the time-zone difference per day. — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)

### Inferences
- **Roster-only CrewCircadian is in the worst regime.** It has no light or activity data, and cabin crew are shift workers with irregular light. The best published equivalent (sleep timing as a phase proxy in shift workers) misses by about 4–6.5 h on average. A plausible honest uncertainty for phase on rotating rosters is **±2–4 h**. On a stable day-type pattern (e.g., a run of daytime short-haul duties with regular sleep) it may be about ±1–1.5 h.
- Adding a phone or watch step-count stream is the single biggest available accuracy upgrade (activity beat wrist light in shift workers). Even then, shift-worker MAE stays at about 2.5 h without individual calibration.
- The tau difference between men and women (and the 35% of women with tau < 24 h) means sex is a legitimate, cheap prior for drift and adaptation direction. It is still a small effect relative to the other errors.
- For crew on 1–2-day long-haul layovers, the safe default is "body clock stays on home-base time." A model assuming fast local adaptation would give wrong light advice.

### Gaps
- Heart-rate-based phase estimation (e.g., Bowman et al. 2021, Cell Reports Methods) and errors in older adults (Mayer et al. 2025, J Sleep Res) were identified but their numbers were not retrieved.
- Chronotype (MSFsc) population distribution figures (Roenneberg) and phase-angle dependence on tau (Wright et al. 2005) were not verified this session.
- No study found validating phase prediction for **cabin crew** or for Indian/Gulf carrier rosters specifically.

## 3. What is the "window of circadian low" formally, where does it come from, and how much does it vary?

### Takeaway
The WOCL comes from NASA Ames' 1996 duty and rest guidelines. It is a **population estimate** ("best estimated by the hours between 0200 and 0600 for individuals adapted to a usual day-wake/night-sleep schedule"), derived from the circadian low of performance, alertness, subjective fatigue and body temperature. Regulators later codified it, typically as 02:00–05:59 in the acclimatised time zone. Because DLMO varies by about ±1.5 h (SD) across adults and drifts with travel and shift work, an individual's true low point can sit well outside the 02:00–06:00 box.

### Cited Findings
- NASA TM-110404 (Dinges, Graeber, Rosekind, Samel, Wegmann; NASA Ames, 1996), §2.3.2, verbatim: "The window of circadian low is best estimated by the hours between 0200 and 0600 for individuals adapted to a usual day-wake/night-sleep schedule. This estimate of the window is calculated from scientific data on the circadian low of performance, alertness, subjective report (i.e., peak fatigue), and body temperature." — [NASA TM 110404](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990063635/downloads/19990063635.pdf?attachment=true)
- Same section, the travel rule: "For flight duty periods that cross 3 or fewer time zones, the window of circadian low is estimated to be 0200 to 0600 home-base/domicile time. For flight duty periods that cross 4 or more time zones… 0200 to 0600 home-base/domicile time for the first 48 hours only. After a crew member remains more than 48 hours away from home-base/domicile, the window… is estimated to be 0200 to 0600 referred to local time at the point of departure." — [NASA TM 110404](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990063635/downloads/19990063635.pdf?attachment=true)
- The same document recommends that if two or more flight duty periods within 7 days encroach on the WOCL, the standard 36-hour weekly off-duty period be extended to 48 hours. — [NASA TM 110404](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19990063635/downloads/19990063635.pdf?attachment=true)
- FAA 14 CFR Part 117 incorporates a WOCL definition. — [eCFR Part 117](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-14/chapter-I/subchapter-G/part-117) (exact wording not retrieved; see Gaps)
- Individual spread: adult DLMO SD is about 1.4–1.6 h (see Section 2). — [PMC10171641](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10171641/)
- Adaptation: pilots on short transpacific layovers stayed on home-base circadian time. — [Gander et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27253676/)
- Acclimatisation fitted best at about 30% per day. — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)

### Inferences
- The WOCL is a **regulatory and group construct**, a fixed clock box tied to an assumed acclimatisation state. It is not a personal measurement. CrewCircadian can use it as a scheduling flag ("this duty touches the WOCL") consistent with FDTL rules. It should not present it as "your body's low point is 02:00–05:59."
- The simple 48-hour rule in NASA 1996 is cruder than the ~30%/day adaptation estimate from Ingre 2014. A product could model a gradual shift, but should show it as a band.

### Gaps
- Exact wording of FAA §117.3, EASA ORO.FTL.105 ("02:00–05:59 in the time zone to which a crew member is acclimatised", from memory, not re-verified), ICAO Doc 9966 and DGCA India CAR definitions was not retrieved this session. These need checking before quoting.
- No source retrieved that quantifies individual CBTmin spread directly. That figure was inferred from DLMO spread.

## 4. Which models are open-source or licensable, and what do Timeshifter, Arcashift, Rise and Entrain say about their models and validation?

### Takeaway
A small team can freely use **Arcascope `circadian`** (MIT; Python; Forger99, Jewett99, Hannay19, Hannay19TP light-driven phase models with DLMO output). It can also use **FIPS** (R; Three-Process Model and Unified Model with KSS and lapse outputs), but FIPS is **AGPL-3.0**, which has network-copyleft implications for a closed app backend. SAFTE-FAST, FAID, BAM and CAS are commercial and proprietary. SAFTE's commercial rights trace to an exclusive US Army license held by Fatigue Science, and SAFTE-FAST is sold by IBR, with Auto-Sleep and light estimation proprietary. Consumer competitors publish **user surveys, not controlled validation**: Timeshifter cites 129,852 post-flight surveys and does not name its model. The only peer-reviewed tests of model-driven shift-work sleep apps (SleepSync) are small uncontrolled pilots.

### Cited Findings
**Open-source**
- Arcascope `circadian`: "License: MIT"; models Forger99, Jewett99, Hannay19 and Hannay19TP; `.dlmos()` phase markers; `LightSchedule` input (e.g., shift-work light patterns); actograms. The README makes no validation or medical claims. Latest release v1.0.2 (Aug 2023), DOI 10.5281/zenodo.8206871, about 19 GitHub stars. — [GitHub Arcascope/circadian](https://github.com/Arcascope/circadian)
- An older Arcascope repository, `hcrsimpy`, simulates human circadian rhythms for a given light schedule. — [GitHub Arcascope/hcrsimpy](https://github.com/Arcascope/hcrsimpy)
- FIPS (Fatigue Impairment Prediction Suite; Wilson, Strickland, Ballard; JOSS 2020): license **GNU Affero GPL-3**. It implements the Three-Process Model (outputs alertness, KSS and components) and the Unified Model (outputs fatigue and PVT lapses), with a Hourly Risk Index in pre-alpha. Input is sleep/wake history. — [GitHub humanfactors/FIPS](https://github.com/humanfactors/FIPS); [JOSS paper](https://joss.theoj.org/papers/10.21105/joss.02340)
- 2B-Alert (US Army, Unified Model + Bayesian individualisation) is published as an app, and a "2B-Alert Web 2.0" was described as "an open-access tool" (preprint). — [Reifman et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.12725); [2B-Alert Web 2.0 preprint](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356241227_2B-Alert_Web_20_An_Open-Access_Tool_for_Predicting_Alertness_and_Optimizing_the_Benefits_of_Caffeine_Preprint)

**Commercial / licensable**
- SAFTE-FAST: "a product of IBR, under license from Fatigue Science." Fatigue Science holds "the exclusive license from the US Army to commercialize SAFTE." ### Inferences
- **Most realistic build path for a small team:** use the MIT `circadian` package (light/activity → phase, DLMO) for light, dark and melatonin timing. Pair it with a simple two- or three-process alertness layer, written in-house from the published equations (avoiding FIPS's AGPL if the backend stays closed) or using FIPS for offline research. Then calibrate against crew diaries. Licensing SAFTE-FAST is possible but aimed at operators. It gives group-average effectiveness, not personal timing, and its sleep imputation is proprietary.
- Timeshifter's "14.1×" figure is observational and confounded (people who follow the advice differ from those who don't; self-reported outcome). It is not evidence of accuracy of timing.
- No competitor has published a validation of **individual phase prediction from a roster alone**. Claiming "pin-perfect" accuracy would exceed anything in the field.

## 5. What can honestly be claimed — minute-level times vs time windows — and how should uncertainty be communicated?

### Takeaway
No published model supports minute-level personal timing from a roster. Validated errors are about ±1 h at best (regular schedules with wearables) and ±2–4 h or worse for shift workers without calibration. Questionnaire-only estimates leave a ~4 h spread. Honest output is **time windows** whose width reflects the data the app actually has, plus rules that are robust to error. The most error-sensitive advice is **light timing near the circadian low**: if the phase estimate is wrong, light can push the clock the wrong way. Experiments show that stating numeric uncertainty (ranges) costs little or no trust, while vague verbal hedging slightly reduces it.

### Cited Findings
- Kantermann et al. 2015 warn that questionnaire-timed light or melatonin "could result in the mistiming of these treatments relative to the DLMO, thereby potentially worsening circadian misalignment." — [Kantermann et al. 2015](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/58750/1/Kantermann_Comparing_the_morningness-eveningness_questionnaire.pdf)
- Shift-worker phase MAE: 2.5–3.8 h for models and 3.9–6.5 h for sleep-timing proxies. Day workers: about 0.6–1.0 h. — [Huang et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/10/zsab126/6278480)
- Sleep prediction from schedule alone overlapped actual sleep 73% of minutes. Pre-shift naps (about 30% of nurses) were not predicted. — [Knock et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/sleep/article/44/11/zsab146/6295924)
- The Three-Process Model can output percentile bands ("reference limits accounting for 50, 75 and 90% of the individuals in addition to the group mean") and a probability of severe sleepiness (KSS ≥ 7). This is an established way to express uncertainty instead of a single line. — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)
- Personalisation can narrow uncertainty: 2B-Alert's predictions improved as it learned from about 12 PVTs. — [Reifman et al. 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jsr.12725)
- Uncertainty communication: across experiments, "communicating uncertainty numerically only exerted a minor effect on trust," while verbal expressions (e.g., "figures could be higher or lower") "slightly decreased" trust. — [van der Bles et al. 2020, PNAS 117(14):7672–7683](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-communicating-uncertainty-on-public-trust-in-facts/); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2020/03/200323152106.htm)
- A joint briefing on how to communicate uncertainty (2020) is available. — [Full Fact briefing](https://fullfact.org/media/uploads/en-communicating-uncertainty.pdf)
- IATA: if inputs to a model are inaccurate, "they will affect the output." Models should be combined with crew perceptions and sleep history. — [IATA HFTF 2025](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/iata_hftf_bmm_limitations_of_fatigue_model_guidance_april_2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Recommended claim structure:**
  1. Sleep and nap *opportunities* derived from the roster can be given precisely (they are facts of the roster minus commute and buffer), e.g., "sleep opportunity 14:10–20:30."
  2. Biological recommendations should be windows, e.g., "best nap window ≈ 13:30–16:00", "avoid bright light roughly 03:00–07:00 home time", with width scaled to confidence: about ±1 h with a stable schedule plus wearable data, about ±2 h or more on rotating or overnight patterns, or fall back to "home-base time" rules.
  3. Avoid strong phase-shifting light prescriptions when uncertainty is high. Prefer robust advice: protect sleep, nap before night duty, caffeine cut-off before planned sleep, darkness during sleep.
- Show *why* (e.g., "based on your last 3 nights and home-base body clock") and collect feedback (sleep diary, KSS, optional 3-minute PVT) to personalise and narrow windows over time, as in the 2B-Alert approach.
- Do not hedge with vague words; state the numeric range.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: direct sleep-app studies on uncertainty display, Joslyn & LeClerc weather-forecast uncertainty experiments, and light PRC papers (Khalsa 2003; St Hilaire 2012) and Eastman & Burgess 2009 on wrong-direction shifts. The "light at the wrong time shifts you the wrong way" point is supported here only by Kantermann's mistiming warning plus general PRC knowledge.

## 6. Risks and value of a single 0–100 fatigue/readiness score in a consumer app

### Takeaway
SAFTE "Effectiveness" is the only well-known 0–100-style fatigue score with operational validation. It is defined as **group-average PVT speed as a % of rested baseline**. Its validation is for group-level accident risk in rail and binned-mean PVT in flight attendants, not for an individual's momentary state. Consumer "readiness" scores are proprietary, inconsistent, and largely unvalidated. A home-grown "SAFTE-like" 0–100 score carries false precision and a misleading implicit "pass/fail" meaning. It could still be useful as a **relative, explained comparison** (e.g., "this pairing is harder than last week's") with bands and no pass/fail thresholds.

### Cited Findings
- SAFTE Effectiveness is "PVT Speed [1/Reaction Time] expressed as a % of individual optimum baseline." The FAA study notes this "only accounts for differences in some baseline parameter… and does not accommodate inherent differences in… sleep need, sleep inertia, or circadian phasing and amplitude." — [Roma et al. 2012](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf)
- Interpretive anchors used by operators: risk rises below 90; below about 77, economic accident risk is about 5×; 70 is "rough equivalent of a 0.08 blood alcohol level or being awake for 21 hours" (rail-derived). — [FAA AM-12/12](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/201212.pdf); [FRA white paper](https://railroads.dot.gov/sites/fra.dot.gov/files/fra_net/2944/fatiguewhitepaper112706.pdf)
- Regulators warn against "qualitative data being misinterpreted as quantitative data" and against using model outputs as the sole go/no-go tool. Model output "does not necessarily correlate to a safety risk." — [CASA guidance](https://www.casa.gov.au/biomathematical-fatigue-models-guidance); [IATA HFTF 2025](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/5f976bb3ca2446f3a40e88b18dd61fbb/iata_hftf_bmm_limitations_of_fatigue_model_guidance_april_2025.pdf)
- Consumer composite scores: a review of 14 composite health scores from 10 manufacturers found "major inconsistencies in metric integration, limited reproducibility." "None of the manufacturers disclosed their exact algorithmic formulas, and few provided empirical validation or peer-reviewed evidence." HRV was used in 86% of scores and resting HR in 79%. — [Doherty et al. 2025, Translational Exercise Biomedicine 2(2):128–144](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/teb-2025-0001/html)
- Even validated models carry about ±1.4 KSS-point residual error for individuals, with a between-person offset SD of 0.84. — [Ingre et al. 2014](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0108679)

### Inferences
- **Risks of the current app's "SAFTE-like" score:**
  - It borrows the credibility of a validated model it does not implement.
  - Integers imply precision far beyond about ±1.4 KSS or 2–4 h phase error.
  - Users may treat it as fit or unfit to fly, which is a safety-relevant decision regulators say models must not make alone.
  - Any threshold (e.g., "below 70 = danger") would be borrowed from US rail data with no cabin-crew equivalence.
- **Safer options:** coarse bands (e.g., low / moderate / high fatigue risk) with an uncertainty note; relative comparisons between days or pairings; and an explanation of the drivers (hours awake, sleep in last 24/48 h, duty overlapping home-time 02:00–06:00). Label it clearly as an estimate for planning, not a fitness-for-duty measure. If the "SAFTE" name or ideas are used, licensing and naming issues apply.
- Validating any score would minimally require prospective crew data (sleep diary or actigraphy plus KSS/PVT across real rosters), as in the FAA study. Report individual-level error, not only binned means.

### Gaps
- Not retrieved: orthosomnia literature (Baron et al. 2017) and the AASM consumer sleep technology position statement (Khosla et al. 2018), which would support the "anxiety and over-reliance" risk.
- I did not check FDA, CDSCO (India) or Gulf regulator positions on wellness vs medical-device claims for fatigue scores.
- I found no published validation of any consumer app's fatigue or readiness score against PVT or KSS in airline crew.
