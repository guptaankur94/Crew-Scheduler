# Chrono-nutrition, meal timing, caffeine, alcohol and hydration for cabin crew (night work and jet lag): evidence as of 2026

Evidence grades used throughout: **Strong** (several RCTs or meta-analyses in humans, consistent results); **Moderate** (one or a few well-controlled human RCTs or lab trials, or consistent findings that are indirect); **Weak** (small, short, or observational human studies, or expert opinion); **Speculative** (animal-only or mechanistic data). Most meal-timing data come from simulated night-shift labs with healthy young non-shift workers. No trial was found that studied cabin crew directly.

---

## 1. Eating at night: glucose tolerance, performance and mood (night work)

### Takeaway
Well-controlled human lab trials agree. Eating a full meal in the biological night (about 00:00–06:00) during night work worsens glucose tolerance and the next-morning glucose response. It also raises sleepiness and worsens performance late in the shift and on the drive home. A small snack (about 10% of daily energy) avoids most of the performance cost and much of the metabolic cost. Eating only in the daytime prevented internal misalignment, glucose intolerance and mood worsening. Defensible app guidance: "Avoid a large meal between about midnight and 06:00 body-clock time. If you're hungry, eat a small snack. Eat your main meal before the duty." Grade: **Moderate**. The trials are consistent and well controlled, but they're small, short, and done in labs with non-shift workers.

### Cited Findings
- **Scheer et al. 2009 (PNAS).** n=10 on a 28-h forced "day" protocol. Eating and sleeping about 12 h out of phase lowered leptin by 17%, raised glucose by 6% despite insulin being 22% higher, reversed the cortisol rhythm, raised mean arterial pressure by 3%, and cut sleep efficiency by 20%. Postprandial glucose reached the prediabetic range in 3 of 8 subjects — [Scheer 2009, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0808180106)
- **Chellappa et al. 2021 (Science Advances).** 14-day circadian paradigm with simulated night work, comparing nighttime eating with daytime-only eating. Nighttime eating misaligned the central rhythm (body temperature) from the peripheral rhythm (glucose) and impaired glucose tolerance. Restricting meals to the daytime prevented both, "despite mistimed sleep" — [Chellappa 2021, Sci Adv (PMC8641939)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abg9910)
- **Qian et al. 2022 (PNAS, same trial).** Night work with day-and-night eating raised depression-like mood by 26.2% and anxiety-like mood by 16.1% compared with baseline. The daytime-only eating group showed no such rise. Greater internal misalignment correlated with worse mood (r=0.77 for depression, r=0.67 for anxiety). The authors call it "proof-of-concept" and say field studies are needed — [Qian 2022, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2206348119)
- **Chellappa et al. 2025 (Nature Communications, secondary analysis, n=20).** In the nighttime-meal group, pNN50 fell 25.7%, RMSSD fell 14.3%, LF/HF rose 5.5% and PAI-1 (a prothrombotic factor) rose 23.9%. The daytime-meal group showed no significant change, and its blood pressure fell 6–8% (exploratory outcome). The authors explicitly mention jet lag as a target — [Chellappa 2025, Nat Commun](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57846-y)
- **Grant et al. 2017 (Chronobiol Int).** 4 simulated night shifts in healthy males. The group that ate at 01:30 (n=4) had a higher glucose AUC after breakfast by night 4 and after returning to day shift. The group that didn't eat at night (n=7) did not. The sample was very small — [Grant 2017](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2017.1335318)
- **Centofanti et al. 2018.** n=20 analysed. A big snack (2100 kJ, about 500 kcal) at 00:00 raised the 08:30 breakfast glucose AUC by 20.8%. A small snack (840 kJ, about 200 kcal) did not — [Centofanti 2018, Neurobiol Sleep Circadian Rhythms](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.nbscr.2017.12.001)
- **Centofanti et al. 2024 (Diabetologia, cluster RCT, n=52).** 4 simulated nights with three arms: fasting 00:30–06:30, a snack (10% of energy) at 00:30, or a meal (30% of energy) at 00:30. Change in OGTT glucose AUC was +0.34 for fasting (ns), +0.96 for the snack (p=0.022) and +2.00 for the meal (p<0.001). Limitations: healthy non-shift-workers, 4 nights only, not powered by sex — [Centofanti 2024, Diabetologia (PMC11663163)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11663163/)
- **Gupta et al. 2019 (Chronobiol Int, same protocol, n=39).** At 04:00 the snack group outperformed both the meal group and the no-eating group on driving simulation, PVT lapses, reaction time and memory. The meal group was sleepiest. "For improved performance, shiftworkers should opt for a snack at night." — [Gupta 2019, Chronobiol Int](https://doi.org/10.1080/07420528.2019.1676256)
- **Gupta et al. 2019 (Nutrients, n=44).** The snack relieved hunger without fullness or extra sleepiness. The no-eating group was hungriest. Gastric upset didn't differ between groups — [Gupta 2019, Nutrients](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu11061352)
- **Gupta et al. 2021 (Scand J Work Environ Health).** On a simulated 07:30 commute after the night shift, the meal-at-night group drove worse (more time outside the safe zone, more lane and speed variability) than the snack and no-eating groups. Snack and no-eating didn't differ — [Gupta 2021, SJWEH (PMC7801136)](https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.3934)
- **Grosser et al. 2026 (Sleep, same RCT).** Both the meal and the snack at 00:30 raised total cortisol output compared with no meal (p=0.019 and p=0.005). Four consecutive nights shifted the cortisol rhythm. Macronutrients were held at about 50% carbohydrate, 33% fat and 17% protein, so composition was not tested — [Grosser 2026, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsaf249)
- **Oriyama & Yamashita 2021 (preprint, n=15 women, crossover).** A 352-kcal snack during a simulated 16-h night shift improved task performance and cut PVT errors compared with skipping. This is a pilot that had not been peer reviewed at that stage — [Oriyama 2021, Research Square](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-334882/v1)
- **2026 systematic review.** Only 4 RCTs in shift workers have tested meal-timing interventions. Night fasting of about 10.75 h lowered glucose AUC compared with eating a meal or snack at night. The authors describe evidence that is scarce, of low methodological quality, and not robust enough to generalise — [Wulandari 2026, Nutr Health](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874)
- **Lowden et al. 2010 review.** It flags "ambiguity about whether shift workers should eat during the night" and says night eating may improve wellbeing while impairing metabolism. The review offers dietary guidelines, which NIOSH later adopted (see Q7) — [Lowden 2010, SJWEH](https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.2898)

### Inferences
- An app can defensibly suggest a **small snack of about 150–250 kcal (≤10% of daily energy) rather than a meal during the biological night**, with the main meal before the duty. Grade: **Moderate**. The absolute kcal figures are my extrapolation from the trial doses of 840 kJ (≈200 kcal) and 10% of energy.
- "Full fasting through the night" helps glucose more than a snack does, but it causes the most hunger and gave no performance benefit over the snack. For safety-critical cabin work, a snack is the better default. Fasting overnight can be offered as an option, not as the rule.
- The window should be anchored to **body-clock time, not local time**. On a layover without adaptation, "midnight to 06:00" means home-base time. This is an inference: the lab studies tested biological night.
- **Macronutrient composition at night:** I found no adequately controlled human trial comparing protein-rich, low-GI and high-carbohydrate night snacks. NIOSH advises avoiding sugar-rich and low-fibre foods, but that is expert opinion. Grade: **Weak**.

### Gaps
- No trials in actual cabin crew, and almost none in real (not simulated) shift workers over the long term.
- No controlled evidence on the best composition of a night snack (protein vs carbohydrate, glycaemic index) for performance or glucose.
- Most samples were young, healthy and largely male. Effects in women, in older crew, and across the menstrual cycle are untested.

## 2. Time-restricted eating (TRE): evidence quality, and whether it suits shift workers

### Takeaway
The Salk Institute (Panda) work is real. However, the striking metabolic protection comes from **mouse** studies, and the human Salk studies are mostly pilots, single-arm trials or modest RCTs. Large human RCTs find that TRE is **no better than ordinary calorie restriction for weight loss**. The 2025 BMJ network meta-analysis rates TRE as roughly equivalent to continuous energy restriction. In shift workers, the only completed TRE RCT (firefighters on 24-h shifts) showed TRE was feasible and safe, with modest benefits mostly in higher-risk people. Grade for "a consistent 10–12-h daily eating window is safe and may help modestly": **Moderate**. Grade for "TRE causes weight loss beyond its calorie effect": **not supported (Strong evidence against)**. The app's "Salk Institute chrono-nutrition" citation overstates the human evidence and should be reworded.

### Cited Findings
- **Hatori et al. 2012 (Salk; mice).** Mice fed a high-fat diet within 8 h/day ate the same calories as mice fed freely, yet were protected from obesity, hyperinsulinaemia and fatty liver. This is **animal-only** evidence — [Hatori 2012, Cell Metab](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2012.04.019)
- **Gill & Panda 2015 (Salk).** In a smartphone-app study, half the cohort ate across more than 14.75 h/day, and fewer than 25% of calories were eaten before noon. A 10–11-h TRE pilot was run in **overweight individuals** (very small, uncontrolled), who lost weight and reported better sleep — [Gill & Panda 2015, Cell Metab](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2015.09.005)
- **Wilkinson et al. 2020 (Salk).** **Single-arm** trial of 10-h TRE for 12 weeks in 19 people with metabolic syndrome. Weight, blood pressure and atherogenic lipids improved — [Wilkinson 2020, Cell Metab](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2019.11.004)
- **Lowe et al. 2020 (TREAT RCT, n=116).** 16:8 TRE (eating 12:00–20:00) for 12 weeks. The between-group difference in weight was −0.26 kg (ns). TRE lost more appendicular lean mass — [Lowe 2020, JAMA Intern Med](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4153)
- **Liu et al. 2022 (NEJM RCT, n=139, 12 months).** Eating 08:00–16:00 plus calorie restriction lost 8.0 kg vs 6.3 kg for calorie restriction alone. The difference of −1.8 kg was not significant (P=0.11), and there were no metabolic differences — [Liu 2022, NEJM](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa2114833)
- **Lin et al. 2023 (Annals RCT, n=90, 12 months).** 8-h TRE without calorie counting lost 4.61 kg vs control and was not different from 25% calorie restriction (difference 0.81 kg, P=0.68) — [Lin 2023, Ann Intern Med](https://doi.org/10.7326/M23-0052)
- **Semnani-Azad et al. 2025 (BMJ network meta-analysis).** 99 RCTs, 6582 adults. All intermittent-fasting strategies and continuous energy restriction reduced weight compared with ad-libitum eating. Only alternate-day fasting beat continuous restriction, by 1.29 kg (moderate certainty). TRE was not superior — [Semnani-Azad 2025, BMJ](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-082007)
- **Manoogian et al. 2022 (Healthy Heroes RCT, 137 firefighters on 24-h shifts, 9% female).** 10-h TRE for 12 weeks narrowed the eating window from 14.13 h to 11.13 h, with no adverse effects and better quality of life. VLDL particle size fell. HbA1c and diastolic BP fell only in participants at elevated baseline risk — [Manoogian 2022, Cell Metab](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmet.2022.08.018)
- **Manoogian et al. 2024 (TIMET RCT, n=108, metabolic syndrome).** 8–10-h TRE on top of standard care improved HbA1c by −0.10% (95% CI −0.19 to −0.003). This is modest, with no major adverse events — [Manoogian 2024, Ann Intern Med](https://doi.org/10.7326/M24-0859)
- **SWIFt trial (Australia).** This is the first RCT of intermittent fasting in night-shift workers with overweight. It compares continuous energy restriction, 2-day/week fasting aligned to night shifts, and 2-day/week daytime fasting. Protocol, qualitative and baseline-diet papers are published. **I found no primary weight-outcome results** — [Davis 2023 protocol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpubh.2023.1228628); [Davis 2025 qualitative](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-025-01750-7); [Clark 2026 baseline diet](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00394-026-03917-9)
- **TRE and female reproductive hormones (2026 systematic review, 6 studies).** RCTs in women with obesity found no change in testosterone, oestrogen, progesterone, LH, cortisol, DHEA or SHBG. Evidence is limited and inconclusive — [Jirapak 2026, Nutr Health](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)

### Inferences
- For crew, the defensible version of "eating windows" is: **keep a consistent eating window of about 10–12 h anchored to your biological day where duty allows, and avoid large meals in the biological night**. Grade: **Moderate**. A strict 8-h or 16:8 window is not supported for performance or weight beyond calorie effects, and it is impractical on irregular rosters.
- Strict TRE carries **risks for crew on duty**: hunger, and pressure to fast across a duty that includes a night sector. TREAT also suggests lean-mass loss, and a strict window can make crew skip galley meals. The app should present windows as guidance, not hard "fasting windows", and should never push fasting through a duty to hit a window.
- **Wording fix for the app:** replace "Salk Institute chrono-nutrition" with something like "Based on human night-shift meal-timing trials (e.g., Brigham & Women's / Univ. of South Australia), plus TRE trials; benefits are mainly metabolic and modest."

### Gaps
- I did not verify the 2024 AHA conference abstract that linked 8-h TRE to higher cardiovascular death (an observational NHANES analysis). It is not cited here, and the report writer should treat it as unverified.
- SWIFt primary outcomes were not found as of this search.
- I found no TRE trial in airline crew, or in people who cross time zones.

## 3. Can meal timing shift the human clock? Pre-flight fasting and the Argonne anti-jet-lag diet

### Takeaway
In humans, meal timing shifts **peripheral** rhythms (the glucose rhythm and the adipose-tissue clock) but **not the master clock** (melatonin and cortisol), at least in the main controlled study. Food is a weak, secondary time cue next to light. The famous "fast 12–16 h before landing to reset the clock" idea rests mainly on **mouse** work (Fuller 2008) and one weak, uncontrolled troop study of the Argonne diet. Grade for "eat meals on destination time to help align gut and metabolic rhythms": **Weak–Moderate**. Grade for "pre-flight fasting resets your body clock and prevents jet lag": **Speculative**.

### Cited Findings
- **Wehrens et al. 2017 (Current Biology, n=10 men).** Delaying all three meals by 5 h for 6 days delayed the plasma glucose rhythm by 5.69 ± 1.29 h and the adipose PER2 rhythm by 0.97 h. It did **not** shift melatonin, cortisol, hunger or sleepiness rhythms, or whole-blood clock genes — [Wehrens 2017, Curr Biol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2017.04.059)
- **Isherwood et al. 2023 (Current Biology, n=24 men).** After 6 days of two large meals, glucose and hunger anticipated the usual mealtimes even under constant routine. Melatonin onset didn't differ between groups. This shows human "food anticipation" without a master-clock shift — [Isherwood 2023, Curr Biol](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.02.005)
- **Lewis et al. 2020 review.** "Although widely assumed to be true, evidence for food zeitgeber effects specific to humans is notably scarce." Food is a time cue "secondary to light" — [Lewis 2020, Nat Rev Endocrinol](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41574-020-0318-z)
- **Fuller, Lu & Saper 2008 (Science).** In **mice**, a Bmal1-based oscillator in the dorsomedial hypothalamus drives food entrainment separately from the light-entrained SCN clock. This work is the basis of the "fast to beat jet lag" idea. It is **animal-only** — [Fuller 2008, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1153277)
- **Reynolds & Montgomery 2002 (Military Medicine).** In 186 National Guard personnel crossing 9 time zones, those who self-selected the Argonne feast/fast diet (4 days before travel) reported less jet lag. The study was not randomised, used self-report and had volunteer bias — [Reynolds 2002, Mil Med](https://doi.org/10.1093/milmed/167.6.451)
- **Chellappa et al. 2025** frame daytime-aligned eating as a countermeasure for misalignment "such as… night work or jet lag". However, they tested simulated night work, not travel — [Chellappa 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-57846-y)
- **Athlete travel consensus (26 experts).** "The lack of evidence restricts the strength of recommendations that are possible." Their toolboxes rely mainly on light and sleep strategies — [Janse van Rensburg 2021, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01502-0)

### Inferences
- Defensible app guidance: on multi-day layovers where adapting is the goal, **eat meals at destination mealtimes** (breakfast after the local morning wake, no big meal in the destination night). Grade: **Weak–Moderate**. It supports peripheral alignment and is low-risk, but don't claim it shifts the master clock.
- On short layovers (≤48 h, common for IndiGo/Air India international sectors and some Gulf turnarounds), crew often should **stay on home time**. Meals should then follow home-base time. This is an inference that follows from the light and sleep strategy, so the meal advice must match the app's "adapt vs. don't adapt" decision.
- **Remove or caveat any "fast X hours before flight to reset your clock" advice.** Fasting before or during duty also conflicts with safety (see Ramadan, Q6).

### Gaps
- I found no randomised human trial of pre-flight fasting or the Argonne diet with objective circadian-phase measures.
- I found no human data on whether **breakfast timing** specifically speeds jet-lag recovery.

## 4. Caffeine: dose, cutoffs, half-life variability, tolerance, caffeine naps, night-work dosing, safe limits

### Takeaway
Caffeine's alertness benefit at night is well supported, and sleep disruption depends on dose and timing. The defensible cutoff guidance is **dose-dependent**: about 100 mg (one coffee) up to about 4 h before sleep is fine for most people (single RCT). A typical coffee is best stopped about 8–9 h before bed (meta-analysis). Large doses (≥200–400 mg) should be stopped 12 h or more before bed. Caffeine disturbs **daytime recovery sleep** (sleep after a night flight) more than night sleep. Safe limits are ≤400 mg/day and ≤200 mg per single dose for adults, and ≤200 mg/day in pregnancy. Half-life varies several-fold between people (OCs about double it; pregnancy greatly prolongs it; smoking roughly halves it). Grades: alertness benefit **Strong**; dose-specific cutoff hours **Moderate**; "caffeine nap" **Moderate**; small repeated doses at night **Moderate**.

### Cited Findings
**Sleep effects and cutoffs**
- **Drake et al. 2013 (n=12).** 400 mg taken at 0, 3 **or 6 h** before bed significantly disturbed sleep. The 6-h dose cut objectively measured total sleep time by more than 1 h — [Drake 2013, J Clin Sleep Med](https://doi.org/10.5664/jcsm.3170); details in [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/11/131114155838.htm)
- **Gardiner et al. 2023 meta-analysis (24 studies).** Caffeine cut total sleep time by 45 min and sleep efficiency by 7%, added 9 min to sleep latency and 12 min of wake after sleep onset, and reduced deep sleep by 11.4 min. Coffee (107 mg) should be taken ≥8.8 h before bed, and a 217.5 mg pre-workout serve ≥13.2 h before bed — [Gardiner 2023, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2023.101764)
- **Gardiner et al. 2025 RCT (Sleep, n=23 males, home PSG).** 100 mg at 12, 8 or 4 h before bed had **no** effect on sleep. 400 mg at 4 h cut total sleep by 51 min. 400 mg at 8 h cut sleep efficiency by 6.9% and added 29 min of wake after sleep onset. 400 mg at 12 h still reduced deep sleep (N3) by 21 min. Authors: "100 mg … up to 4 hours prior to bedtime", and "400 mg … should not be consumed within 12 hours of bedtime" — [Gardiner 2025, Sleep (PMC11985402)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985402/)
- **Carrier et al. 2007 (n=34).** 200 mg (100 mg at 3 h and 100 mg at 1 h before bed) reduced sleep efficiency more strongly during **daytime recovery sleep** than during night sleep. It reduced sleep duration and REM only in the daytime-sleep group — [Carrier 2007, Neuropsychopharmacology](https://doi.org/10.1038/sj.npp.1301198)
- **EFSA:** about 100 mg "may affect sleep duration and patterns in some adults, particularly when consumed close to bedtime" — [EFSA caffeine topic](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine)
- **Burke et al. 2015 (Sci Transl Med).** A double-espresso dose taken 3 h before bedtime delayed the melatonin rhythm by about 40 min, which is about half the delay from 3 h of bright evening light. **Caffeine is itself a clock-shifting cue** — [Burke 2015](https://doi.org/10.1126/scitranslmed.aac5125)

**Night-work and jet-lag dosing**
- **Cochrane review (13 RCTs).** Caffeine reduced errors and improved some cognitive domains in jet lag or shift work. No trial measured injuries — [Ker 2010, Cochrane](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD008508)
- **Wyatt et al. 2004.** Low doses repeated hourly (0.3 mg/kg/h, ≈20 mg/h for 70 kg) attenuated performance decline, "particularly at the circadian performance nadir" — [Wyatt 2004, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/27.3.374)
- **Schweitzer et al. 2006.** Lab n=68 and field n=53 shift workers. An evening nap plus caffeine was the best combination for night-shift alertness and PVT performance — [Schweitzer 2006, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/29.1.39)
- **Beaumont et al. 2004.** After a 7-time-zone eastbound flight, 300 mg slow-release caffeine at 08:00 reduced daytime sleepiness but "tended to affect sleep quality" — [Beaumont 2004, J Appl Physiol](https://doi.org/10.1152/japplphysiol.00940.2002)
- **US Army 2B-Alert / Vital-Lopez 2024.** These are model-based tools that optimise caffeine dose and timing around sleep loss, built on the validated Unified Model of Performance — [Vital-Lopez 2024, Sleep](https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/zsae133); [Reifman 2022, JMIR](https://doi.org/10.2196/29595)

**Caffeine nap**
- **Horne & Reyner 1996.** A nap under 15 min and 150 mg caffeine each reduced driving impairment in sleepy drivers. A break alone didn't help — [Horne & Reyner 1996](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1996.tb00428.x)
- **Hayashi et al. 2003.** 200 mg caffeine taken just before a 20-min nap was the most effective condition for sleepiness and performance for 1 h after waking — [Hayashi 2003, Clin Neurophysiol](https://doi.org/10.1016/s1388-2457(03)00255-4)

**Variability, tolerance, safe limits**
- **Half-life.** 3–7 h in adults. OCs "tend to double the half-life". In pregnancy it is on average 8.3 h longer and up to 16 h longer. Smoking doubles clearance. No marked difference between luteal and follicular phases. Heavy alcohol use slows clearance — [Temple 2017, Front Psychiatry (PMC5445139)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5445139/)
- **CYP1A2 metabolises about 95% of caffeine.** Polymorphisms in CYP1A2 and in the ADORA2A receptor drive differences in pharmacokinetics and sensitivity between people — [Nehlig 2018, Pharmacol Rev](https://doi.org/10.1124/pr.117.014407)
- **Tolerance.** Daily 3 mg/kg kept a performance effect for about 15–18 days, but the effect progressively shrank (exercise outcome, n=11) — [Lara 2019, PLoS One](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0210275)
- **EFSA 2015.** Up to 400 mg/day (≈5.7 mg/kg) and single doses up to 200 mg (≈3 mg/kg) raise no safety concerns for healthy adults. Up to 200 mg/day is safe in pregnancy and lactation — [EFSA topic page](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine); [EFSA Journal 2015;13(5):4102](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2015.4102)
- **FDA.** 400 mg/day for most adults, with "wide variation" in sensitivity and elimination. A 12-oz brewed coffee contains 113–247 mg, black tea 71 mg and energy drinks 41–246 mg. About 1,200 mg taken rapidly can cause seizures — [FDA consumer update](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/spilling-beans-how-much-caffeine-too-much)
- **Pregnancy outcomes.** In the NICHD cohort (n=2055), the highest plasma caffeine quartile was linked to lower birth weight (−84 g) and shorter length. This was seen even at intakes below 200 mg/day, so it is observational and not causal — [Gleason 2021, JAMA Netw Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2021.3238)
- **Cabin crew survey (n=930).** Caffeine use was independently associated with insomnia and shift-work disorder risk (cross-sectional) — [Wen 2020, IJERPH](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18010013)

### Inferences (possible app rules)
- **Make the cutoff depend on dose, not a single clock time.** Suggested rules: about 100 mg (one cup) no later than 4–6 h before planned sleep; 200 mg or more no later than 8–10 h before; 400 mg no later than about 12 h before. Grade: **Moderate**. For sleep after a night flight (daytime sleep), use the stricter end, since caffeine disrupts daytime sleep more (Carrier 2007).
- **Front-load caffeine on a night duty.** Use small doses (about 50–100 mg) early to mid-duty, especially before the 03:00–06:00 trough, and stop in the last part of the duty when a post-flight sleep is planned. Grade: **Moderate** (Wyatt, Schweitzer, and the cutoff data).
- **Caffeine nap.** About 100–200 mg taken right before a nap of 20 min or less, during in-flight rest or before driving home. Grade: **Moderate** (small lab studies, consistent results).
- **Personalisation flags.** Users on OCs or who are pregnant should get earlier cutoffs, since half-life roughly doubles or more. Pregnancy should also cap caffeine at 200 mg/day. Smokers clear caffeine faster. Grade: **Moderate** (pharmacokinetic evidence is strong; the cutoff translation is inferred).
- **Caffeine affects clock timing.** Evening caffeine can delay the clock (about 40 min), which matters when trying to phase-advance for eastward travel. Grade: **Moderate** (single RCT).
- **Indian context.** Chai is the main caffeine source. ICMR-NIN advises no tea or coffee 1 h before or after meals because tannins inhibit iron absorption (see Q6).

### Gaps
- I didn't verify the ACOG wording (commonly cited as <200 mg/day in pregnancy); EFSA's 200 mg/day was verified.
- I found no caffeine-content data for Indian chai or filter coffee specifically.
- The cutoff data come mostly from young men sleeping at night. Effects on daytime sleep and in women are less well characterised.

## 5. Alcohol on layovers; hydration in the cabin

### Takeaway
Even about 2 standard drinks reduce REM sleep, and alcohol shifts sleep toward more fragmentation in the second half of the night. Alcohol during or just before in-flight sleep combines with cabin hypoxia to lower SpO2 and raise heart rate. Indian regulation already bars alcohol for cabin crew within 12 h before flight duty. Grade for "avoid alcohol as a sleep aid, especially before a rest that precedes duty": **Strong** for sleep architecture. On hydration: cabin humidity is usually below 20%, which dries skin, eyes and airways. WHO says there's no evidence low humidity causes internal dehydration. Mild dehydration (about 1.5–2% of body mass) does impair mood, attention and vigilance, so regular drinking to thirst and normal urine colour is reasonable. Grade for "drink regularly during duty": **Moderate**. Grade for "the cabin itself dehydrates you": **Weak or contested**.

### Cited Findings
- **Gardiner et al. 2025 meta-analysis (27 studies).** Alcohol delays REM onset and reduces REM duration from a low dose (≤0.50 g/kg ≈ 2 drinks), with a dose-response. Faster sleep onset was seen only at high doses (≥0.85 g/kg ≈ 5 drinks), which "likely exacerbates subsequent REM sleep disruption" — [Gardiner 2025, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030)
- **Ebrahim 2013 review.** At all doses alcohol shortens sleep latency and consolidates the first half of the night, but increases disruption in the second half — [Ebrahim 2013, Alcohol Clin Exp Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006)
- **Pietilä et al. 2018 (n=4098, real-world HRV).** Alcohol dose-dependently reduced parasympathetic activity and physiological recovery during the first 3 h of sleep, even at low intake (≤0.25 g/kg) — [Pietilä 2018, JMIR Ment Health](https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519)
- **Trammer/Elmenhorst et al. 2024 (Thorax, DLR).** Altitude chamber at the 2438 m cabin equivalent, BAC about 0.043%. Alcohol plus hypobaria lowered SpO2 during sleep to a median 85.3% (88.1% hypobaria alone; 95.9% at ground level) and raised heart rate to 87.7 bpm (72.9 hypobaria alone). Participants spent about 201 min below 90% SpO2, and N3 sleep fell to 46.5 min — [Trammer 2024, Thorax](https://doi.org/10.1136/thorax-2023-220998); [DLR release](https://www.dlr.de/en/me/latest/news/2024/study-shows-drinking-alcohol-on-long-haul-flights-could-affect-the-health-of-sleeping-passengers)
- **DGCA (India).** No cabin crew member "shall have taken or used any alcoholic drink, sedative, narcotic or stimulant drug preparation within 12 hours of the commencement of the flight" (Aircraft Rules 1937, Rule 24). Crew get mandatory pre-flight breath-analyser tests — [DGCA notification via TeamLease RegTech, Sept 2023](https://www.teamleaseregtech.com/updates/article/26621/dgca-notified-the-procedure-for-medical-examination-of-aircraft-person/); [DGCA document](https://www.dgca.gov.in/digigov-portal/Upload?flag=iframeAttachView&attachId=YPPvmAUI%2FXKRXSX6YN5FfA%3D%3D)
- **Cabin crew survey.** Use of alcohol or drugs for sleep was independently associated with insomnia and shift-work disorder risk — [Wen 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18010013)
- **WHO International Travel and Health.** Cabin humidity is "usually less than 20%". Low humidity can cause skin, eye, mouth and nose dryness, but "the available evidence has not shown low humidity to cause internal dehydration and there is no need to drink more than usual". WHO advises limiting caffeine and alcohol on long flights. Quote taken via search snippet of this PDF; recheck against the current edition — [WHO ITH (PDF copy)](https://imhf-portal.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/WHO-International-Travel-and-Health.pdf)
- **Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018 meta-analysis (33 studies).** Dehydration impaired cognition with a small overall effect (ES −0.21). Attention (−0.52), executive function and motor coordination were most affected, with more impairment above 2% body-mass loss — [Wittbrodt 2018, MSSE](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001682)
- **Armstrong 2012 (women, −1.36% body mass).** Mood, concentration, perceived task difficulty and headache all worsened — [Armstrong 2012, J Nutr](https://doi.org/10.3945/jn.111.142000)
- **Ganio 2011 (men, −1.59% body mass).** More vigilance errors, slower working memory, and more fatigue and tension — [Ganio 2011, Br J Nutr](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114511002005)
- **Aircrew survey (n=101).** 50% reported limited access to nutritious food on duty. 71% reported sleep disturbance and 89% fatigue — [Stefańska 2025, Nutrients](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17243906)

### Inferences (possible app rules)
- Show the **regulatory alcohol window** (DGCA: 12 h before the duty start; check GCAA/airline rules for Emirates and Etihad) as a hard rule. Separately, give a sleep-quality message that even 1–2 drinks reduce REM and recovery. Grade: **Strong**.
- Advise against alcohol before **in-flight crew-rest sleep**. Grade: **Moderate** (one chamber study, healthy young people). Crew can't drink on duty anyway, but this matters for deadheading or positioning crew.
- **Hydration.** Sip water regularly through duty, aim for pale urine, and drink more in hot climates and on Gulf layovers. Avoid claims that the cabin "dehydrates you by X litres". Grade: **Moderate** for cognition effects; **Weak** for cabin-specific fluid needs.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring the hydration status of cabin crew across real sectors.
- I didn't fetch the EFSA reference values for total water intake (commonly cited as 2.0 L/day for women and 2.5 L/day for men), so treat these as unverified here.
- I didn't verify GCAA (UAE) alcohol rules for crew.

## 6. User-base context: galley food, Indian diets, Ramadan with night flying, women's health

### Takeaway
Crew report poor access to nutritious food on duty, so advice must work with galley meals and packed snacks. For Indian crew, chai and coffee timing interacts with iron absorption: ICMR-NIN advises no tea or coffee within 1 h of meals, which matters for largely vegetarian diets. Ramadan reduces sleep by about 1 h/night and shifts sleep later. At least one operator (PIA) has advised crew not to fast while flying, and a crash inquiry said fasting "probably impaired" pilot judgement. The app should treat fasting crew as a special mode, not a default "fasting window". Pregnancy needs a caffeine cap of 200 mg/day and no TRE or fasting advice. Grade: **Moderate** for the caffeine and iron guidance; **Weak** for the Ramadan-on-duty guidance, which has no trials in crew.

### Cited Findings
- **Galley and access.** 50% of surveyed airline personnel had limited access to nutritious food during duty, and 60% reported weight fluctuations — [Stefańska 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17243906)
- **ICMR-NIN Dietary Guidelines for Indians (2024).** Avoid tea or coffee at least 1 h before and after meals, because tannins bind iron and can cause iron deficiency and anaemia. Excess coffee raises blood pressure — [NewsOnAir/ICMR release](https://newsonair.gov.in/icmr-releases-updated-dietary-guidelines-for-indians-in-2024); [Medindia summary](https://www.medindia.net/news/lifestyleandwellness/trouble-for-tea-and-coffee-fans-icmrs-shocking-revelation-215734-1.htm)
- **Ramadan sleep meta-analysis (24 studies).** Total sleep fell from 7.2 h to 6.4 h and the Epworth sleepiness score rose slightly, from 6.1 to 7.0 — [Faris 2020, Sleep Breath](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11325-019-01986-1)
- **BaHammam review.** When sleep schedule and light are controlled, Ramadan fasting does not alter circadian rhythms or cognition. In real life, bedtimes and wake times are sharply delayed and REM proportion falls — [Qasrawi 2017, Sleep Breath](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11325-017-1473-x)
- **PIA.** Pakistan International Airlines instructed pilots and cabin crew not to fast while flying during Ramadan. The PK8303 inquiry (2020 A320 crash) said "judgement of both flight crew was probably impaired due to the effects of fasting while flying", but the effect on performance "could not be determined" — [Arab News](https://www.arabnews.com/node/2476511/pakistan); [FlightGlobal](https://www.flightglobal.com/safety/fasting-pia-crews-judgement-probably-impaired-but-direct-link-to-a320-crash-undetermined/157126.article)
- **Ramadan and caffeine.** Fasting-related headache is traditionally attributed to dehydration, caffeine withdrawal and sleep disruption. A 2026 14-country survey suggests pre-existing headache disorders also predict it — [Akgor 2026, Front Neurol](https://doi.org/10.3389/fneur.2026.1859536)
- **Ramadan in pregnancy (22 observational studies).** Fasting did not affect birth weight or preterm birth, but evidence on other outcomes is insufficient. Pregnant women are religiously exempt — [Glazier 2018, BMC Pregnancy Childbirth](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-018-2048-y)
- **Caffeine pharmacokinetics in women.** OCs roughly double the half-life, pregnancy prolongs it by about 8–16 h, and there are no marked menstrual-phase differences — [Temple 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5445139/). EFSA sets ≤200 mg/day in pregnancy and lactation — [EFSA](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine)
- **TRE and female hormones.** RCT evidence shows no hormonal changes in women with obesity, but the evidence is limited — [Jirapak 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261467643)

### Inferences (possible app rules)
- **Galley-aware advice.** Suggest carrying a small snack (e.g., nuts, yogurt, fruit, a wholegrain sandwich or roti roll, or a protein option for vegetarians) for the biological-night sector, and eating the hot crew meal before the night trough rather than at 02:00–04:00 body time. Grade: **Weak–Moderate** (inferred from Q1 and NIOSH).
- **Indian-specific.** Separate chai or coffee from meals by about 1 h, especially for vegetarian women at risk of anaemia, and count chai toward the caffeine budget. Grade: **Moderate** (national guideline plus well-known physiology).
- **Late Indian dinners.** On days off, nudge the main meal earlier, before about 2–3 h ahead of sleep. Grade: **Weak**. I found no Indian-specific meal-timing outcome data.
- **Ramadan mode.** Don't schedule "fasting windows" that clash with religious fasting, and don't give religious rulings. Do flag safety: crew should follow their airline's or regulator's policy on fasting during duty. Encourage suhoor close to dawn and protecting sleep. Taper caffeine before Ramadan to avoid withdrawal headaches; this taper advice is an **inference**. Grade: **Weak**.
- **Pregnancy mode.** Cap caffeine at 200 mg/day, apply an earlier caffeine cutoff, and switch off TRE and fasting suggestions. Grade: **Moderate** for the caffeine cap.

### Gaps
- I found no published evidence on Ramadan fasting by cabin crew, or on GCAA, Emirates or Etihad Ramadan crew policies.
- I found no Indian studies of meal timing in crew or shift workers, and no data on caffeine content of chai.
- I didn't verify NFHS-5 anaemia prevalence figures.

## 7. Consensus and guideline documents on shift-work nutrition, and how precise they are

### Takeaway
Official guidance is consistent but deliberately imprecise: avoid or reduce eating between midnight and 06:00, keep a normal day pattern, eat light at night, keep caffeine within limits, and stay hydrated. The BDA explicitly says the evidence "is not yet strong enough to make firm recommendations" on timing. A 2021 review of 26 online guidelines found inconsistencies on snacking frequency. No body gives hour-level eating windows or caffeine cutoffs specific to shift workers. Grade: **Weak** (expert opinion). An app giving minute-precise "eating windows" is more precise than any guideline body supports.

### Cited Findings
- **NIOSH (CDC) nurse training, Module 9.** "Avoid eating or reduce food intake between midnight and 6 a.m. Use the normal day and night pattern of meal timing as much as possible, and divide eating into three meals per 24-hour period… Avoid sugar-rich products and low-fiber carbohydrate foods. These can increase sleepiness." Attributed to Lowden et al. — [NIOSH Module 9](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod9/08.html)
- **British Dietetic Association (2022).** "The evidence is not yet strong enough to make firm recommendations as to the optimal timing of eating in shift workers but there may be some benefits in": having the main meal earlier, before the shift; taking lighter meals and snacks at night "(rather than avoiding food & drink at night)"; and eating a light meal 1–2 h before bed. Caffeine should be kept "within recommended levels" (no numbers given) — [BDA](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/nutrition-shift-work.html)
- **D'Annibale et al. 2021 review (26 guidelines).** Advice covered eating patterns (25 of 26), caffeine control (23) and hydration (17). There were inconsistencies on frequency of eating and snacking, and the authors note a need for evidence-based guidance — [D'Annibale 2021, Nutrition Bulletin](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nbu.12515)
- **2026 systematic review.** Only 4 meal-timing RCTs exist in shift workers — [Wulandari 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/02601060261464874)
- **Athlete jet-lag consensus.** Lack of evidence restricts the strength of recommendations — [Janse van Rensburg 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-021-01502-0)
- **EFSA and FDA caffeine limits.** These are general-population safety limits, not performance guidance — see Q4.

### Inferences
- The app should match guideline precision. Use soft windows ("aim to avoid a big meal 00:00–06:00 body time"), framed as **recommendations with an evidence badge**, not prescriptions. Caffeine cutoffs can be more specific because there is dose-timing RCT data.

### Gaps
- I didn't retrieve the Sleep Health Foundation (Australia) or HSE (UK) shift-work pages in full.
- I found no aviation-regulator (ICAO, EASA, DGCA, GCAA) nutrition guidance for cabin crew.
- I found no formal Delphi consensus on shift-work nutrition as of 2026.
