# Recovery after early-start duties and using the gap before next-day standby (aviation evidence and official guidance)

Scope note: this extends `research_notes/Cabin crew circadian app direction/aviation_cabin_crew_fatigue.md`, which covers the FMG 2015 personal-countermeasure tables, the NASA long-haul nap study and the DGCA cabin crew CAR. This file adds primary text from EASA Regulation (EU) 83/2014 (ORO.FTL), the EASA AMC/GM to Subpart FTL (Amendment 3, ED Decision 2014/003/R), the EASA FTL effectiveness studies (FTL1/FTL2.0), more FMG 2015 wording on standby, napping, early starts and crew responsibility, and the short-haul pilot early-start studies (Roach 2012, Arsintescu 2022, NASA, Vejvoda 2014).

**Evidence base caution:** almost all quantified early-start evidence is from **pilots**, not cabin crew. I found no cabin-crew study that measures sleep before early starts or naps after early duties. Where cabin crew are covered, it says so.

Worked scenario used throughout: report 05:00, home about 17:00, airport or home standby from 12:00 the next day.

---

## 1. How much sleep is lost before early starts, and do crew nap after early duties?

### Takeaway
In short-haul pilots, the earlier the report, the less sleep beforehand:
- About 5.4 h in the 12 h before a 04:00–05:00 start, against 6.6 h before a 09:00–10:00 start. That is roughly 15 minutes lost for each hour the start moves earlier than 09:00.
- Pilots wake around 04:19 for early starts but cannot go to bed much earlier.

Field studies record naps after early duties only sparsely. I found no aviation study that measures how a post-early-duty nap affects sleep the following night.

### Cited Findings
- **Roach et al. 2012** (short-haul pilots, n = 70, at least 2 weeks of diaries plus wrist activity monitors):
  - "The amount of sleep obtained in the 12 h prior to duty was lowest for duty periods that commenced between 04:00 and 05:00 h (i.e. 5.4 h), and greatest for duty periods that commenced between 09:00 and 10:00 h (i.e. 6.6 h)."
  - About 15 min of sleep is lost "for every hour that the start of duty is advanced prior to 09:00 h."
  - Self-rated fatigue at the start of duty was highest for 04:00–05:00 starts.
  - The authors recommend an FRMS with "fatigue-friendly" rosters, plus training so that pilots can "optimize sleep opportunities".
  - — [Roach GD, Sargent C, Darwent D, Dawson D. Duty periods with early start times restrict the amount of sleep obtained by short-haul airline pilots. Accid Anal Prev 2012 (CQU repository)](https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Duty_periods_with_early_start_times_restrict_the_amount_of_sleep_obtained_by_short-haul_airline_pilots/13464182/1); also indexed by [SWOV](https://swov.nl/nl/publicatie/duty-periods-early-start-times-restrict-amount-sleep-obtained-short-haul-airline-pilots)
- **Arsintescu et al. 2022** (short-haul pilots; 95 recruited, 94 analysed; 36 days; 1,476 FDPs; "early" = start 05:00–06:59):
  - Sleep before early starts was 6.94 h (SE 0.10), against 8.47 h before late finishes (p < 0.001).
  - Table 2 gives mean wake time before early starts as 04:19 (SD 1:14) and bedtime as 21:15 (SD 2:01).
  - "Participants reported obtaining less sleep than their average sleep need when duty started early" (mean self-reported need was 7.76 h).
  - Early starts gave worse fatigue and performance **before** duty. Late finishes gave worse fatigue and performance at **end** of duty.
  - Prior sleep duration was not a significant predictor once time awake was accounted for.
  - On naps: "Naps were taken 0.08% of times across all duty days and were usually taken after early starts." The figure is reported as printed and looks implausibly low; it may be a typo for 8% or a proportion.
  - The authors say strategies to "increase pilot sleep duration prior to early starts should be evaluated," and that countermeasures including napping need testing.
  - — [Arsintescu L et al. Early starts and late finishes both reduce alertness and performance among short-haul airline pilots. J Sleep Res 2022;31(3):e13521 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9286802)
- **NASA Ames short-haul study** (44 pilots, actigraphy plus diaries; baseline, early, midday and late schedules):
  - Total sleep time during early starts was 5.70 h, against 6.78 h at baseline.
  - Sleep stayed low across consecutive early-start days.
  - This comes from a search summary of the NTRS record. The full paper was not read, so verify before quoting.
  - — [NASA NTRS 20190001653](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20190001653)
- **Vejvoda et al. 2014** (40 short-haul pilots):
  - Time awake was the main predictor of fatigue.
  - Late-finishing pilots had been awake about 5.5 h longer at duty start than early starters.
  - This comes from a search summary and the full text was not read.
  - — [DLR elib 90472](https://elib.dlr.de/90472)
- **EASA FTL1 study** (pilots, European operators):
  - All disruptive schedules raised the probability of high fatigue (KSS ≥ 7) at the last top of descent compared with daytime FDPs: range 0.22–0.42, against 0.16. Early starts were defined as a start between 05:00 and 06:59.
  - The increase was "less evidently so for early start FDPs."
  - "An earlier start time predicted an increased probability (odds ratio) of high fatigue during the early start FDPs."
  - Naps (most likely controlled rest) occurred in 31% of measured long night duties and "14% of the other measured disruptive schedules."
  - — [EASA research project EASA.2019.C31, Deliverable 2.5, Effectiveness of Flight Time Limitations (FTL2.0), NLR](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/141964/en)
- **ULR cabin crew (not early starts):** 95% of cabin crew took a daytime nap on the first day home after an Auckland–Chicago ULR trip, and first-night sleep was longer (9.9 h). This shows cabin crew do use post-duty naps, but in a long-haul recovery context. — [Frontiers in Environmental Health 2023, ULR cabin crew study](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/environmental-health/articles/10.3389/fenvh.2023.1329206/pdf)
- **CAMI flight attendant field study (US, 202 FAs):** "seemingly few, if any, flight attendants begin their workday at their well-rested best." This is from a search summary of the FSF article. — [Flight Safety Foundation ASW Dec 2010–Jan 2011](https://flightsafety.org/asw/dec10-jan11/asw_dec10-jan11_p26-29.pdf)

### Inferences
- Before a 05:00 report, a crew member is likely to sleep about 5–6 h (pilot data), waking around 03:30–04:15 depending on commute. By 17:00 they will have been awake roughly 13 h on a sleep deficit of 1.5–2.5 h relative to need.
- The pilot data apply reasonably to cabin crew on the same report time. Cabin crew report times and post-flight duties are similar or longer, and the mechanism (circadian timing of sleep) is not job-specific. Treat the numbers as estimates for cabin crew.
- No aviation field study tests whether a late-afternoon nap after an early duty shortens or delays the following night's sleep. Any app rule on nap length or timing after early duties rests on general sleep science, not aviation evidence.

### Gaps
- Bourgeois-Bougrine et al. (2003, French short/medium-haul pilots), Flower (2001), Sallinen et al. (Finnish short-haul), and Powell et al. (2007, NZ short-haul) were not retrieved in this pass.
- An ICAO regional slide attributes "early starts" as a fatigue cause to Powell et al. (2007), but this is secondary. — [ICAO-ACAO FRMS Webinar 2022](https://www.icao.int/MID/Documents/2022/FRMS%20Webinar/2022.06.09%20ICAO-ACAO%20FRMS%20Webinar.pdf)
- No cabin-crew study quantifying sleep before early starts or naps after early duties was found.
- No study was found on the effect of a post-early-duty nap on the next night's sleep in crew.

## 2. What official guidance says about naps, recovery after early starts, and standby

### Takeaway
None of the official documents read gives an explicit "after an early duty, do X" recommendation. The most specific guidance is the FMG 2015 (IATA/ICAO/IFALPA):
- Some sleep is always better than none.
- The afternoon nap window is about 15:00–17:00.
- The evening wake maintenance zone makes it hard to sleep earlier than usual.
- Recovering from a sleep debt takes two full nights in a row.
- On standby: "When feeling sleepy while waiting for call-out, attempt sleep (prioritize sleep over other activities)".

EASA's rules are about rostering, not personal tips. They require standby to be pre-notified "to provide them with the opportunity to plan adequate rest". Flight time specification schemes must take into account "the interference of standby with sleep" and "sufficient notification to protect a sleep opportunity between the call for duty and the assigned FDP".

### Cited Findings
**FMG 2015, the IATA/ICAO/IFALPA Fatigue Management Guide for Airline Operators, 2nd ed.** All quotes below come from the [same FMG PDF](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf). The text was extracted locally from the PDF.
- **Afternoon nap window (Glossary):** "A time of increased sleepiness in the middle of the afternoon. The precise timing varies, but for most people it is usually around 15:00-17:00. This is a good time to try to nap. On the other hand, it is also a time when it is more difficult to stay awake, so unintentional micro-sleeps are more likely, especially if recent sleep has been restricted."
- **Evening wake maintenance zone (Glossary):** "A period of several hours in the circadian body clock cycle, just before usual bedtime, when it is very difficult to fall asleep."
- **Operational Implication 7, early starts:**
  - "Whenever a duty period overlaps an individual's usual sleep time, it can be expected to restrict sleep. Examples include early duty start times, late duty end times, and night work."
  - "The evening wake maintenance zone occurs in the few hours before usual bedtime. This makes it very difficult to fall asleep earlier than usual, ahead of an early duty report time. Early report times have been identified as a cause of restricted sleep in aviation operations."
  - "Across consecutive duty periods that result in restricted sleep, individuals will accumulate a sleep debt and fatigue-related impairment will increase. To recover from a sleep debt, individuals need a minimum of two full nights of sleep in a row."
- **Sleep loss and recovery (section 2.2):**
  - "Lost sleep is not recovered hour-for-hour, although recovery sleep may be slightly longer than normal sleep at night. At least two consecutive nights of unrestricted sleep are required for the non-REM/REM sleep cycle to return to normal."
  - On the first recovery night more slow-wave sleep occurs. On the second, "the brain catches up on REM sleep."
- **Operational Implication 5, napping:**
  - "If a crew member has been awake for a long period of time or if he/she has not had enough sleep over one or more days, some sleep is always better than none. Napping is a mitigation that can help maintain performance and alertness in the short term, until a full sleep opportunity is available and utilised."
  - "Napping before duty: When a duty period starts later in the day (e.g. in the evening or at night) a nap prior to commencing work will reduce the period of wakefulness."
  - "It is also critical that individuals are educated to not reduce their sleep time in anticipation of a nap during a flight or duty period."
  - The guidance on letting sleep inertia dissipate before duty is in Operational Implication 1.
- **Operational Implication 4, standby, reserve and on-call** (addressed to operators):
  - "Although standby, reserve and on-call duties lack the certainty associated with scheduled shifts, the same scientific principles still apply."
  - Protocols should "Minimize interruptions during circadian times when sleep is more likely… interruptions (such as non-urgent phone calls from work) during those times should be minimised."
  - They should also "Minimize continuous hours of wakefulness before and during duty periods that are unscheduled. When being called-in is highly likely, establishing minimal notification periods before the individual can be asked to report for duty allows the opportunity for some sleep. If minimal notification periods are not operationally feasible, an extended duty is required or a call-back occurs late in the day or during the night, naps will reduce increasing sleep pressure over extended waking hours."
  - And "Build in some level of schedule predictability… the time of day for potential duty should be predictable and consistent… allows for individuals to plan and manage their sleep periods."
- **Table 4-1, unpredictable call-outs (personal strategies):** "Ensure that sleep environment is dark and quiet, and use sleep hygiene measures to maximize sleep quality. Maximize recovery sleep on off-duty days. When feeling sleepy while waiting for call-out, attempt sleep (prioritize sleep over other activities)."
- **Operator responsibility:** management should provide "flight and cabin crew with adequate opportunity for recovery sleep between duties." The FMG also lists as a hazard "not having the opportunity for adequate recovery sleep after one trip (or set of consecutive duties) before starting the next trip."

**EASA: Regulation (EU) No 83/2014 amending 965/2012, Annex III Subpart FTL.** All quotes below come from the [same text, LawPlayer transcription of the regulation](https://lawplayer.com/eu/act/32014R0083). The official source is EUR-Lex CELEX 32014R0083.
- **ORO.FTL.225, standby and duties at the airport:**
  - "(a) standby and any duty at the airport shall be in the roster and the start and end time of standby shall be defined and notified in advance to the crew members concerned to provide them with the opportunity to plan adequate rest"
  - "(c) airport standby shall count in full as duty period for the purpose of points ORO.FTL.210 and ORO.FTL.235"
  - "(e) the operator shall provide accommodation to the crew member on airport standby"
  - Under (f)(2), schemes must specify the impact of standby on maximum FDP, "taking into account facilities provided to the crew member to rest, and other relevant factors such as: — the need for immediate readiness of the crew member, — the interference of standby with sleep, and — sufficient notification to protect a sleep opportunity between the call for duty and the assigned FDP."
- **Definitions (ORO.FTL.105):**
  - "'standby' means a pre-notified and defined period of time during which a crew member is required by the operator to be available to receive an assignment for a flight, positioning or other duty without an intervening rest period"
  - "'airport standby' means a standby performed at the airport"
  - "'other standby' means a standby either at home or in a suitable accommodation"
  - "'local night' means a period of 8 hours falling between 22:00 and 08:00 local time"
  - "'window of circadian low ('WOCL')' means the period between 02:00 and 05:59 hours in the time zone to which a crew member is acclimatised."
- **ORO.FTL.235(e):** schemes "shall specify additional rest periods… to compensate for… (2) additional cumulative fatigue due to disruptive schedules."
- **EASA FAQ 47648:** if an early duty is a standby or airport duty leading to an FDP, the preceding rest after a late or night duty must include a local night. This is from a search summary; verify the wording on the FAQ page. — [EASA FAQ 47648](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/faq/47648)

**EASA AMC/GM to Subpart FTL** (Annex to ED Decision 2014/003/R, Amendment 3, 31 Jan 2014). Text extracted locally from the PDF; some characters in special fonts were lost. All quotes below come from the [same PDF](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/16778/en).
- **AMC1 ORO.FTL.110(b)(3), scheduling:** "preparation of duty rosters sufficiently in advance with planning of recurrent extended recovery rest periods and notification of the crew members well in advance to plan adequate pre-duty rest."
- **AMC1 ORO.FTL.110(b)(1), scheduling:** operators should avoid "undesirable practices such as alternating day/night duties… so that a serious disruption of established sleep/work patterns occurs."
- **AMC1 ORO.FTL.110(a), publication of rosters:** "Rosters should be published 14 days in advance."

**EASA FTL2.0 study, standby (pilots):** "self-rated fatigue during FDPs assigned during these [other-than-airport] standby periods was associated more closely with the amount of prior sleep and the sleep-to-awake time ratio than the time spent awake per se. However, these results were largely based on non-disruptive assignments only." The report also discusses an "18 hours awake time cap in the context of other-than-airport standby." — [EASA FTL2.0 D2.5](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/141964/en)

**UK CAA:** search snippets indicate that UK CAA guidance on ORO.FTL.110 recommends fatigue awareness training so crew "are aware of what could affect their ability to rest for their duties." The CAA also states that its guidance is its own interpretation and EASA's may differ. I did not read the full documents. — [UK CAA operator responsibilities (2014)](https://www.caa.co.uk/media/2w1pn4nx/20140124operatorresponsibilities.pdf); [UK CAA publication 15061](https://caa.co.uk/publication/download/15061)

### Inferences
- Applied to the scenario (home about 17:00, standby from 12:00 the next day), the guidance supports these points:
  - Arriving home at 17:00 falls at the end of the FMG's 15:00–17:00 afternoon nap window. A nap that day would start at or just after that window, so it would need to be short and early enough not to eat into the evening sleep drive. This is an inference: the FMG gives no explicit cut-off.
  - The main recovery tool is a long, unrestricted night. Because standby starts at 12:00, there is no reason to set an early alarm, so a full night plus a natural wake is possible. The FMG says lost sleep "is not recovered hour-for-hour" and that full recovery takes two nights.
  - During home standby, the FMG says to "attempt sleep (prioritize sleep over other activities)" when sleepy. EASA FTL2.0 found that fatigue on duties assigned from home standby tracks **prior sleep**, not just time awake. Both support protecting the night before standby.
  - Airport standby counts in full as duty under EASA, and the operator must provide accommodation. Home standby is treated differently (see the DGCA section below).
- EASA's 18 h awake cap discussion is relevant if the standby (from 12:00) leads to a late call. A crew member who wakes at 08:00 could be pushing 18 h awake by about 02:00. This supports a late-standby nap if sleepy, as FMG Operational Implication 4 suggests.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the text of CS FTL.1.225 (EASA certification specification for standby). This covers the maximum home-standby duration (recalled as 16 h), the standby-plus-FDP awake-time limit (recalled as 18 h), and the night-time standby counting rule. The FTL2.0 report confirms that an "18 hours awake time" cap exists in the context of other-than-airport standby. The other values are unverified recall.
- No EASA, UK CAA or FMG text addresses naps specifically **after** early duties.
- The full text of ICAO Doc 9966 (2nd ed. 2016) was not retrieved. The FMG is the operator companion and reproduces the same science, but Doc 9966 wording was not confirmed.
- UK CAA fatigue guidance (for example CAP 1265 or the CAA's FTL guidance pages) was not read in full.

## 3. Verification of claims (a) and (b)

### Takeaway
- **Claim (a), ICAO cabin-crew fatigue guidance lists five factors:** only partly verified.
  - ICAO's fatigue definition, which ICAO applies to both flight and cabin crew, names sleep loss, extended wakefulness, circadian phase and workload.
  - "Duty duration" and "duty pattern" as named factors in a cabin-crew-specific ICAO text were not found.
  - No standalone ICAO cabin-crew fatigue guide exists. The FMG says its examples "focus on flight crew."
- **Claim (b), EASA guidance on disruptive schedules, sleep-wake history and personalised advice:**
  - That EASA recognises disruptive schedules is verified, in binding rule text.
  - That EASA "discusses sleep-wake history" is verified only in EASA-commissioned research (FTL1/FTL2.0), not in rule or AMC/GM.
  - "Personalised fatigue mitigation advice" was not found in any EASA text.

### Cited Findings
- **ICAO definition:** fatigue is "a physiological state of reduced mental or physical performance capability resulting from sleep loss or extended wakefulness, circadian phase, or workload." The provisions are applicable to flight and cabin crew. This is from search summaries of ICAO's Cabin Crew Fatigue Management page, which could not be fetched (DNS failure and HTTP 403). — [ICAO Cabin Crew Fatigue Management](https://www.icao.int/cabin-crew-fatigue-management); [ICAO archived page](https://www2023.icao.int/safety/airnavigation/OPS/CabinSafety/Pages/Cabin-Crew-Fatigue-Management.aspx)
- **ICAO cabin crew documents:** ICAO's cabin crew page points to Doc 9966, the IATA/ICAO/IFALPA implementation guide and the Cabin Crew Safety Training Manual (Doc 10002). This is from a search summary. — [ICAO Cabin Crew Fatigue Management](https://www.icao.int/cabin-crew-fatigue-management)
- **FMG scope caveat:** "flight crew fatigue has received much more scientific, operational, and regulatory attention than cabin crew fatigue, so the examples in this manual focus on flight crew." — [FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf), as in the background notes
- **FMG hazard examples close to "duty duration/pattern":** "high total duty time and flight time over specified periods… which increases the risk of cumulative fatigue; not having the opportunity for adequate recovery sleep after one trip (or set of consecutive duties) before starting the next trip." — [FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **EASA, disruptive schedule definition (ORO.FTL.105(8)):** "'disruptive schedule' means a crew member's roster which disrupts the sleep opportunity during the optimal sleep time window by comprising an FDP or a combination of FDPs which encroach, start or finish during any portion of the day or of the night where a crew member is acclimatised. A schedule may be disruptive due to early starts, late finishes or night duties."
  - "'early type'… (i) for 'early start' a duty period starting in the period between 05:00 and 05:59"

  - — [Reg. (EU) 83/2014](https://lawplayer.com/eu/act/32014R0083)

  - "(b) the basics of fatigue including sleep fundamentals and the effects of disturbing the circadian rhythms";
  - "(e) fatigue countermeasures";
  - "(f) the influence of lifestyle, including nutrition, exercise, and family life, on fatigue";
  - "(h) where applicable, the effects of long range operations and heavy short range schedules on individuals";
  - "(j) the crew member responsibility for ensuring adequate rest and fitness for flight duty."
  - — [EASA AMC/GM Subpart FTL](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/16778/en)
- **EASA research, sleep-wake history:**
  - FTL1/FTL2.0 used sleep logs recording "bed time, lights off time, wake-up time, sleep quality, feelings of restedness, and sleep location."
  - "shorter sleep and long prior awake time measured by a sleep log were significant predictors of high fatigue."
  - The deliverable carries a disclaimer that it expresses the contractor's opinion, not EASA's.
  - — [EASA FTL2.0 D2.5](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/downloads/141964/en)
- **Personalisation:** a search for EASA guidance on personalised mitigation from sleep-wake history found nothing. The nearest items are EASA's Best Intervention Strategy BIS15 "Aircrew Fatigue (SI-0039)" (2025), which is not read, and commercial tools such as Jeppesen CrewAlert. — [EASA FTL report news](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/newsroom-and-events/news/easa-report-effectiveness-flight-time-limitation-recommends-enhancing); [Blue Sky News on CrewAlert](https://www.blueskynews.aero/issue_218/jeppesen_crewalert_ios_app_helps_crew_members_perform_optimally.htm)

## 4. DGCA India: standby rules relevant to sleep planning

### Takeaway
Under the in-force cabin crew CAR 7-J-I Issue IV:
- Standby must be notified at least 12 h ahead and lasts at most 12 h, of which at most 8 h at the airport.
- Airport standby counts 100% towards FDP if it leads to a flight.
- Home standby does not count if the crew member is called in the first 6 h.

For the scenario, notification of a 12:00 standby should arrive by 00:00, which in practice is the published roster. The rest after an uncalled standby is 8 h (home) or 12 h (airport).

### Cited Findings
These rules come from the background notes and a third-party transcription of CAR 7-J-I Issue IV (effective 01-Nov-2018). The official DGCA PDF was not retrieved. — [Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf); [Wingmanlog summary](https://www.wingmanlog.in/post/understanding-cabin-crew-fdtl-guidelines-dgca-car-7-j-i)
- **Standby duration:** "Maximum 12 h, of which at most 8 h at the airport."
- **Airport standby:** it counts 100% towards FDP if it leads to a flight.
- **Home or hotel standby:**
  - None of it counts if the crew member is called within the first 6 h.
  - If called at 6 h or later, the FDP is reduced by 50% of the standby time.
  - If not called, 25% counts as duty.
- **Rest after uncalled standby:** 8 h for home standby, 12 h for airport standby.
- **Notice:** standby must be notified at least 12 h ahead.
- **Reserve:** it needs notification "at least 10 hours in advance." Standby has no intervening rest.
- **Local night:** "eight hours falling between 2200 hours and 0800 hours."
- **Minimum rest before an FDP:** the greater of the preceding duty or 12 h, within 3 time zones.
- **Crew duty:** crew "shall plan and use rest periods to ensure that they are fully rested" and shall not operate if fatigued. The operator "shall not require" a crew member "known or suspected" to be fatigued to operate.
- **Status of the replacement CAR:** draft Issue V remains unfinalised as of August 2026 per Wingmanlog. — [Wingmanlog](https://www.wingmanlog.in/post/understanding-cabin-crew-fdtl-guidelines-dgca-car-7-j-i)

### Inferences
- In the scenario, the gap from release (about 15:30–16:30 if the FDP ends then; actual release depends on the post-flight allowance) to the 12:00 standby is about 19–20 h. That is well above the 12 h minimum rest, and it includes a full local night (22:00–08:00).
- Home standby from 12:00 could, if called after 18:00, lead to an FDP running deep into the night. The FDP reduction applies only to calls at 6 h or later. Sleeping in the morning before standby, and napping during standby if sleepy, are the levers the crew member controls.
- Whether standby is at the airport or at home matters to the app:
  - Airport standby counts as duty.
  - Sleep at the airport depends on the facilities provided. EASA requires accommodation for airport standby; the DGCA requirement was not verified.

### Gaps
- The official DGCA PDF for Issue IV standby wording was not retrieved.
- The DGCA pilot CAR standby rules and draft Issue V standby changes were not re-checked in this pass.
- Whether DGCA requires accommodation for airport standby was not found.

## 5. Self-management tools cannot establish fitness for duty; crew must report fatigue

### Takeaway
All the regulatory texts read put the duty on the crew member to use rest to sleep, to arrive fit for duty, and to report fatigue. They also put the duty on the operator to have a "not fit for duty" process.
- EASA: the crew member must "make optimum use of the opportunities and facilities for rest provided and plan and use their rest periods properly."
- FMG: crew are responsible for "arriving fit for duty… and for reporting fatigue hazards."

No source found says explicitly that a self-management app "cannot establish fitness for duty." The closest wording is that fitness is the crew member's own assessment, made within the operator's reporting process.

### Cited Findings
- **EASA ORO.FTL.115, crew member responsibilities:** "Crew members shall: (a) comply with point CAT.GEN.MPA.100(b) of Annex IV (Part-CAT); and (b) make optimum use of the opportunities and facilities for rest provided and plan and use their rest periods properly." — [Reg. (EU) 83/2014](https://lawplayer.com/eu/act/32014R0083)
- **CAT.GEN.MPA.100(b):** this is the referenced Part-CAT provision on crew not performing duties when unfit, including through fatigue. Its exact text was not retrieved in this pass.
- **FMG 2015, crew responsibility:** the quotes below come from the [same FMG PDF](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf).
  - "Crew members are responsible for arriving fit for duty, including making appropriate use of rest breaks to obtain sleep, and for reporting fatigue hazards."
  - "Individual crew members are responsible for: making optimum use of off-duty periods to get adequate sleep; coming to work fit for duty; managing their own fatigue levels; reporting fatigue issues."
- **FMG reporting:** "The operator needs to have a process for reporting 'not fit for duty' due to fatigue, and a clear procedure around the consequences." The operator should also "Establish a process for what to do when a crew member considers that they are too fatigued to perform safety-critical tasks to an acceptable standard." — [FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **FMG sample FRMS policy ("Flight and cabin crew are required to"):** "inform their manager or supervisor immediately prior to or during work if: they know or suspect they or another crew member are suffering from unacceptable levels of fatigue; or they have any doubt about their or another crew member's capability to accomplish the[ir duties]." — [FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Self-assessment:** "people tend to become less reliable at assessing their own level of impairment" as sleepiness increases. — [FMG 2015, Glossary](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Training on tools:** for roster staff, training should cover the "use and limitations of any scheduling tools and bio-mathematical [models]." — [FMG 2015](https://www.iata.org/contentassets/39bb2b7d6d5b40c6abf88c11111fcd12/fatigue-management-guide_airline20operators.pdf)
- **Models:** an EASA webinar slide cautions that a bio-mathematical model is not an FRM. This is from a search summary. — [EASA node 74880](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/node/74880)
- **DGCA:** crew "shall plan and use rest periods to ensure that they are fully rested" and shall not operate if fatigued. This is from a third-party CAR copy. — [Issue IV copy](https://understandingdgacfdtl2012.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/dgca-fdtl-cc-2018.pdf)

### Inferences
- An app can support the crew member's obligation to plan and use rest. Its wording should mirror ORO.FTL.115(b) and the FMG.
- It should not present any output as a fitness-for-duty determination:
  - Fitness is the crew member's own judgement, reported through the operator's process.
  - The FMG notes that self-assessment degrades as sleepiness rises.
  - Even EASA treats bio-mathematical models as not being an FRM on their own.
- A suitable app line would be: "If you feel too tired to work safely, use your airline's fatigue reporting process."

### Gaps
- No regulator text was found that explicitly addresses consumer or self-management apps and fitness for duty.
- The CAT.GEN.MPA.100(b) wording was not retrieved.
