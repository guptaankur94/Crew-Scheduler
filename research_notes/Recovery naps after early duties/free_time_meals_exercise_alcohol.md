# Using free time between duties when sleep-restricted: meals, exercise, alcohol, light/screens, social time

Scenario: early-start duty, home about 17:00, tired, short prior sleep; next duty is a 12:00 standby. The goal is to protect the next main sleep (roughly 21:30–07:30) and arrive at 12:00 alert.

Evidence grades: **Strong** (meta-analyses or several consistent human RCTs); **Moderate** (a few controlled human trials, or large consistent observational data); **Weak** (small, observational, lab-only or indirect); **Speculative** (mechanistic or extrapolated). "[LAB]" means healthy volunteers in a lab, not shift workers. This extends `Cabin crew circadian app direction/exercise_light_melatonin_naps.md` (Q2, Q3) and `nutrition_caffeine.md` (Q1, Q5, Q7). Those findings are summarised here, not re-searched.

---

## 1. Evening exercise when tired: intensity × timing, injury risk, and whether a walk is fine

### Takeaway
Light-to-moderate exercise in the evening does not harm sleep, and a walk is fine (**Strong** for "moderate evening exercise is not harmful"). Vigorous or maximal exercise ending within about 1–2 h of bedtime can delay sleep onset and shorten sleep; ending ≥4 h before sleep shows no association (**Moderate**). Sleep loss lowers exercise performance, especially in the afternoon and evening, so a hard session after an early duty brings less benefit (**Moderate**). Evidence that sleep loss raises injury risk is mixed in adults (**Weak**). The rule "don't recommend hard workouts to someone significantly fatigued; a light walk is fine if alert" is **supported as cautious advice**. The performance-loss part is evidence-based, and the timing-before-bed part is meta-analytic. The injury-risk part is plausible but not proven in adults.

### Cited Findings
- **Stutz et al. 2019 meta-analysis (23 studies).** Evening exercise slightly increased slow-wave sleep (+1.3 percentage points) and REM latency (+7.7 min). The studies "do not support the hypothesis that evening exercise negatively affects sleep". The exception: sleep-onset latency, total sleep time and efficiency "might be impaired after vigorous exercise ending ≤1 h before bedtime" — [Stutz 2019, Sports Med](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-1015-0) (from prior notes)
- **Frimpong et al. 2021 meta-analysis (15 acute studies, n=194).** High-intensity exercise ending 0.5–4 h before bed reduced REM by 2.34%, with no other significant changes. Exercise 2–4 h before bed did not disrupt sleep in healthy adults aged 18–50 — [Frimpong 2021, Sleep Med Rev](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34416428) (from prior notes)
- **Leota et al. 2025.** 14,689 wearable users, 4,084,354 person-nights, observational. Compared with light exercise, maximal exercise ending 2 h before habitual sleep onset was associated with 36.0 min later sleep onset, 22.2 min (5.4%) shorter sleep, 6.8% higher resting HR and 14.1% lower HRV. Exercise ending ≥4 h before sleep showed no association at any strain. The paper notes that guidelines suggest moderate exercise ending ≥90 min before bed won't disrupt sleep — [Leota 2025, Nat Commun](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12000559/) (from prior notes)
- **Craven et al. 2022 meta-analysis (69 publications, n=959, 89% male).** Sleep loss (≤6 h) reduced exercise performance by −7.56% (95% CI −11.9 to −3.13), about 0.4% more per extra hour awake. Restriction by waking early was harmful. Morning tasks were largely unaffected; afternoon/evening tasks were consistently impaired. The authors advise prioritising morning exercise when sleep loss can't be avoided — [Craven 2022, Sports Med](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35708888) (from prior notes)
- **Injury risk, adolescents.** The Gao et al. 2019 meta-analysis found chronic lack of sleep associated with greater sports and musculoskeletal injury risk. It said evidence "cannot yet definitively determine the effect of acute lack of sleep on injury rates", and all included studies used self-reported sleep — [Gao 2019 (PMC6446394)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6446394)
- **Injury risk, adults.** A 2021 systematic review of 12 prospective cohorts concluded the evidence "does not support poor sleep as an independent risk factor" for sport or training injury in adult athletes. It cited methodological heterogeneity — [Springer Medicine summary of 2021 systematic review](https://www.springermedicine.com/the-association-between-poor-sleep-and-the-incidence-of-sport-an/22256538). A separate 2021 BJSM-indexed review concluded that poor sleep quantity and/or quality have "a negative effect on the prevalence of sports injuries" — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4074350?lng=en). **The sources conflict.**
- An often-quoted figure ("<7 h sleep for ≥14 days → 1.7× injury risk") comes from adolescent cohort work. I could not trace it to an adult meta-analysis, so don't use it for adult crew — [secondary blog repeating it](https://dentalsleeppractice.com/sleep-deprivation-and-increased-risk-of-sports-related-injuries/)
- **Feeling alert vs being alert.** A 30-s sprint after waking reduced subjective sleepiness but did not improve cognitive performance — [Kovac 2021](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/34606883) (from prior notes)

### Inferences
- **Evening after an early duty (home 17:00, bed about 21:30–22:00):**
  - A light walk or easy mobility, ideally outdoors before dusk, is fine. Grade: **Strong** that it won't harm sleep. Its benefit for recovery itself is **Weak** (not directly studied).
  - Moderate exercise is fine if it ends ≥90 min before bed. Grade: **Moderate**.
  - Discourage vigorous or maximal sessions. Bedtime is only about 4–5 h away, so a session finishing after about 18:00 enters the window where Leota sees later, shorter sleep. Performance is also lowest in the evening after sleep loss. Grade: **Moderate**.
- **Morning before a 12:00 standby:** if the person wants a harder session, the morning after a full night's sleep is the better slot. Craven found morning tasks least affected, and it is far from the next sleep. Grade: **Moderate** (performance), **Weak** (crew-specific).
- **Recommended wording for the rule:** "If you feel very tired, skip the hard workout today. A gentle walk is fine and may help you unwind. Save hard training for after a full night's sleep." Avoid claiming "you'll get injured". Injury risk from acute sleep loss in adults is unproven, so say it "may be higher". Because sleepy people misjudge their alertness (Kovac), the app shouldn't let "I feel fine" override this after a very short sleep. Grade: **Moderate** overall.
- Driving to and from a gym when sleep-deprived is a separate, better-established risk. That evidence is outside this note.

### Gaps
- No study tested evening exercise in people who are already sleep-restricted (as opposed to rested) and measured the next night's sleep.
- No adult RCT or strong cohort data on acute (one-night) sleep loss and exercise injury. No crew-specific data.
- No study compares a walk vs rest on recovery sleep after an early duty.

## 2. Meal timing and size before sleep (large, late, high-fat, spicy meals; hunger)

### Takeaway
The evidence that a late dinner harms sleep in healthy adults is weaker and more mixed than popular advice suggests. In a randomized crossover PSG study, an isocaloric dinner 1 h before bed did not change sleep latency, efficiency or whole-night architecture compared with dinner 5 h before. It did shift sleep to deeper early and lighter later. Observational data link high-fat, high-calorie intake close to bed with worse sleep. Very spicy food may disrupt sleep via body temperature (one tiny study). For the scenario, a practical, low-regret rule is: eat the main meal soon after getting home (about 17:30–19:00), keep it moderate and not very fatty or spicy, and don't go to bed hungry (a small snack is fine). Grade: **Weak–Moderate**. The "2–3 h cutoff" is a convention, not a proven threshold.

### Cited Findings
- **Duan et al. 2021 (randomized crossover, inpatient PSG, n=20, ages 18–30) [LAB].**
  - Routine dinner at 18:00 (about 5 h before bed) vs late dinner at 22:00 (1 h before bed). Isocaloric meals, about 50% carbohydrate, 35% fat, 15% protein; dinner was 35% of daily energy.
  - No significant differences in sleep latency, efficiency, total sleep time or whole-night stage distribution.
  - The late dinner raised delta power early (+2.5 points), with lighter sleep later: more stage 2 in the second quarter (49.0% vs 36.5%) and more REM in the third quarter (28.1% vs 21.9%). Arousals didn't differ.
  - Authors' conclusion: the late dinner "did not adversely change overall sleep architecture".
  - Source: [Duan 2021, Nat Sci Sleep (PMC8131073)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8131073)
- **Crispim et al. 2011 (cross-sectional, n=52, PSG plus 3-day food diaries).**
  - Nocturnal intake (dinner plus late snack) was typically eaten about 30–60 min before bed.
  - In women (n=15), the nocturnal calorie share correlated with longer sleep latency (r=0.67) and lower sleep efficiency (r=−0.51).
  - In adjusted pooled models, nocturnal **fat** was associated with lower sleep efficiency (β=−0.17, p=0.004), more WASO (β=0.55, p=0.01), longer REM latency and less REM. Nocturnal calories alone were not significantly associated with latency or efficiency.
  - Limitations: the abstract and the results conflict on the sex-specific findings, and there was no correction for multiple comparisons.
  - Source: [Crispim 2011, J Clin Sleep Med (PMC3227713)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3227713/)
- **Other observational data (secondary summary, original papers not fetched).**
  - A cross-sectional study of college students linked a last meal within 3 h of bedtime to more night awakenings.
  - A Japanese young-adult study linked shorter dinner-to-bed intervals to longer sleep latency.
  - Source: summarised in [Frontiers in Nutrition 2024 TRE–sleep review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2024.1419811/pdf). Treat as **Weak**.
- A widely quoted "Brigham trial: meal 4 h vs 6 h before sleep → 26 min longer to fall asleep, 9% lower efficiency" **could not be verified** against a primary paper. Do not cite it.
- **Time-restricted eating trial (Cienfuegos 2021, n=49 completers).** Eating only 15:00–19:00 or 13:00–19:00 for 8 weeks did not change self-reported sleep quality, duration, latency or insomnia severity vs controls — [Cienfuegos 2021, Nutr Health (PMC8460695)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8460695). An early last meal is not harmful to sleep, but it brings no clear benefit either.
- **Spicy food (Edwards et al. 1992, about 6 young men) [LAB].** Tabasco and mustard added to dinner. Secondary summaries report less slow-wave and stage 2 sleep, longer sleep latency and more wake, attributed to higher body temperature in the first sleep cycle. The original paper was not accessed — [Int J Psychophysiol 1992;13:97–100, via summary](https://www.psychologie.uni-heidelberg.de/ae/allg/lehre/essen2003/spicy.pdf). Grade: **Weak**.
- **Guidelines.** The BDA (UK) says evidence is "not yet strong enough" for firm shift-work meal-timing advice, but suggests "a light meal 1–2 h before bed" — [BDA](https://www.bda.uk.com/resource/nutrition-shift-work.html) (from prior notes). NIOSH says to avoid sugar-rich and low-fibre carbohydrate foods, which can increase sleepiness — [NIOSH Module 9](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod9/08.html) (from prior notes).
- Night-time large meals impair glucose tolerance and performance compared with snacks (Centofanti 2024, Gupta 2019) [LAB, simulated night work]. This is about eating in the biological night, not an evening dinner — see `nutrition_caffeine.md` Q1.

### Inferences
- **Scenario guidance:**
  - Eat a proper, moderate meal soon after getting home (about 17:30–19:00), aiming to finish about 2–3 h before bed. Grade: **Weak–Moderate**. It is low-risk and fits the observational data and the BDA, but Duan shows a late meal isn't clearly harmful.
  - If dinner ends up late, a normal-sized meal is still acceptable. Avoid a very large, high-fat or very spicy meal in the last 1–2 h. Grade: **Weak**.
  - Don't go to bed hungry: a small snack (e.g., yoghurt, fruit, toast or a small roti, a handful of nuts) is fine. Grade: **Weak** (guideline-level; no RCT on hunger and sleep onset found).
- The app should phrase this as "earlier and lighter is a bit better", not as a hard cutoff. That matches guideline precision and avoids making tired crew skip food (see the adherence notes in Q5).
- **Morning before the 12:00 standby:** eat a normal breakfast and a meal before reporting. A heavy meal right before report could add post-meal sleepiness — inferred from NIOSH and the night-meal trials. Grade: **Speculative–Weak**.

### Gaps
- No RCT has compared meal timing before an *early* bedtime (about 21:30) in sleep-restricted people.
- No meta-analysis of dinner-to-bed interval and sleep was found.
- No study on hunger at bedtime and sleep onset was found.
- The Edwards 1992 and Brigham "4 vs 6 h" details are unverified.

## 3. Alcohol: dose effects on sleep, recovery after sleep loss, and aviation rules

### Takeaway
Even 1–2 drinks reduce REM and physiological (HRV) recovery during the first hours of sleep. Higher doses speed sleep onset but fragment the second half of the night. Sleep loss and alcohol add together for sleepiness: sleep loss in hours was 2.7× as potent as ethanol in g/kg on MSLT sleep latency. A sleep-restricted person who drinks is therefore sleepier and more impaired than either factor alone would make them. Grade: **Strong** for sleep-architecture harm; **Moderate** for additive sleepiness. Aviation alcohol rules (FAA 8 h, DGCA 12 h, plus BAC limits) are legal floors, not sleep advice. For a 12:00 standby, DGCA's 12 h rule means no alcohol after 00:00. The sleep evidence argues for none at all that evening.

### Cited Findings
- **Gardiner et al. 2025 meta-analysis (27 studies).** REM onset delayed and REM duration reduced from low doses (≤0.50 g/kg ≈ 2 drinks), dose-dependently. Faster sleep onset appeared only at ≥0.85 g/kg (about 5 drinks) — [Gardiner 2025, Sleep Med Rev](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.smrv.2024.102030) (from prior notes)
- **Ebrahim 2013 review.** At all doses, alcohol shortens sleep latency and consolidates the first half of the night but increases disruption in the second half — [Ebrahim 2013, ACER](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006) (from prior notes)
- **Pietilä et al. 2018 (n=4,098, real-world HRV).** Alcohol dose-dependently reduced parasympathetic activity and physiological recovery during the first 3 h of sleep, even at low intake (≤0.25 g/kg) — [Pietilä 2018, JMIR Ment Health](https://doi.org/10.2196/mental.9519) (from prior notes)
- **Sleep loss and ethanol compared (Roehrs et al.) [LAB].** Both reduced MSLT sleep latency as a linear function of dose. "Sleep loss in hours" was 2.7× as potent as "ethanol in g/kg" for sedation, and comparable for psychomotor impairment — [PubMed 14746378](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14746378/)
- **Roehrs & Roth 2001 review.** Alcohol "interacts with sleep deprivation and sleep restriction to exacerbate daytime sleepiness and alcohol-induced performance impairments". The 1996 study gave 0.5 g/kg after 4 h vs 8 h time in bed (n=12 men); its full results were not retrieved — [Roehrs & Roth 2001 via citation](https://www.unboundmedicine.com/medline/citation/11584549/full_citation); [1996 study (scite)](https://scite.ai/reports/sleep-restriction-ethanol-effects-and-x2jlWQ)
- **Recovery sleep after sleep loss.** In men with alcohol *dependence*, the normal slow-wave-sleep rebound after partial sleep deprivation was blunted — [Irwin et al. 2002, Biol Psychiatry PDF](https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/April%202002%20-%20Sleep%20Deprivation%20as%20%20a%20Probe%20of%20Homeostatic%20Sleep.pdf). I found **no** human study of social drinking by healthy people during recovery sleep after sleep restriction.
- **Rules.**
  - FAA 14 CFR 91.17: no acting as a crewmember "within 8 hours after the consumption of any alcoholic beverage", or with alcohol concentration ≥0.04. The 8 h is a floor, not a guarantee — [14 CFR 91.17 text](https://rotatepilot.com/regulations/14-cfr-91-17)
  - DGCA India: no alcohol within 12 h of the start of flight duty, plus pre-flight breathalyser tests — [via prior notes / TeamLease RegTech](https://www.teamleaseregtech.com/updates/article/26621/dgca-notified-the-procedure-for-medical-examination-of-aircraft-person/)
  - EU: random testing of flight and cabin crew under Reg. (EU) 2018/1042. A secondary source gives 0.2 g/L blood as the impairment threshold (not verified against EUR-Lex) — [migflug summary](https://migflug.com/afterburner/?p=25045994)
- **Cabin crew survey.** Using alcohol or drugs for sleep was independently associated with insomnia and shift-work-disorder risk — [Wen 2020](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18010013) (from prior notes)

### Inferences
- **Scenario:**
  - Advise no alcohol the evening before the standby. Grade: **Strong** on sleep grounds.
  - If the person drinks anyway: keep it to one drink, finish it early in the evening with food, never use it as a sleep aid, and always show the hard regulatory cutoff (DGCA: 12 h before the earliest possible report). On a standby, the earliest possible call-out time may be what matters, so the app should take the standby start, not the actual report.
- Do not present "8 h / 12 h" as "safe for sleep". It is only the legal minimum. Grade: **Strong**.
- When sleep-restricted, the same drink produces more sleepiness, and a drink plus a short sleep compounds next-day impairment. Grade: **Moderate** (lab MSLT).

### Gaps
- No human trial of moderate drinking during recovery sleep after acute restriction.
- The full 1996 Roehrs result is not retrieved.
- EASA and UAE GCAA crew alcohol hours are not verified.
- Whether DGCA's 12 h counts from the standby start or the call-out is not verified; check the operator manual.

## 4. Evening light and screens; morning daylight before a midday duty

### Takeaway
Bright, blue-enriched screen light in the hours before bed can suppress and delay melatonin and add about 10 min to sleep onset. That comes from a 4-h-per-night lab exposure [LAB]. Real-world screen use shows small effects in adults: experts did not reach consensus that screens impair adult sleep. Dim room light matters as much as the screen itself: even 10–50 lux in the evening measurably delays the clock. Daytime and morning daylight is associated with better mood and sleep. For the scenario, an early bedtime makes evening light more important, because the person is trying to sleep earlier than usual. Grade: **Moderate–Strong** for "dim the room lights in the last 2–3 h"; **Weak–Moderate** for "screens specifically ruin sleep"; **Moderate** for "get outdoor daylight in the morning".

### Cited Findings
- **Chang et al. 2015 (PNAS, n=12, crossover) [LAB].**
  - Reading on a light-emitting eReader for about 4 h before bed on 5 consecutive evenings vs a printed book in dim light.
  - Results: about 10 min longer sleep latency, about 11 min less REM, more than 55% melatonin suppression, melatonin onset delayed by about 1.5 h, less evening sleepiness and more morning sleepiness.
  - Sources: [PMC4313797](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4313797/); [Penn State release](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/light-emitting-e-readers-detrimentally-shift-circadian-clock)
- **Evening dim light sensitivity (Phillips et al. 2019).** Group ED50 for melatonin suppression was 24.6 lux. 10, 30 and 50 lux delayed melatonin onset by 22, 77 and 109 min. Individual ED50 ranged from 6 to 350 lux. The Brown et al. 2022 consensus recommends ≤10 lux melanopic EDI for ≥3 h before bed, ≥250 lux by day and ≤1 lux in the bedroom (from prior notes, `exercise_light_melatonin_naps.md` Q3).
- **Real-world screens.**
  - A National Sleep Foundation expert panel reached consensus that screen use impairs sleep in children and adolescents, **but not for adults** — [summary via JMIR/lenz search](https://www.jmir.org/2024/1/e48356)
  - A 2025 cohort meta-analysis (21 cohorts, about 548,000 people) found each extra hour of daily screen time associated with about 3–5 min shorter sleep (OR for short sleep 1.25). Observational; reverse causation is possible — [Front Psychiatry 2025](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2025.1640263/text)
- **Daylight and mental health (Burns, Windred et al., UK Biobank, about 86,000 people, 7-day wrist light sensors).** Higher night-time light was associated with higher risk of depression, anxiety, PTSD, psychosis, bipolar disorder and self-harm. Higher daytime light was associated with lower odds of depression, PTSD, psychosis and self-harm, independent of sleep and activity. Observational — [medRxiv preprint](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2022.10.16.22280934v2); [PsyPost summary of Nat Mental Health paper](https://www.psypost.org/individuals-spending-more-time-in-daylight-have-lower-risk-of-mental-health-disorders/)
- **Office field studies.** Morning blue/high circadian-stimulus light was associated with earlier waking and more consolidated rest-activity (n=19, 3 weeks, LRC/GSA). Workers with daylight at their desks had longer sleep (n=50, actigraphy). Indoor electric light is usually about 300–400 lux vs 1,000–10,000+ lux outdoors. Small, partly self-report — [RPI news](https://news.rpi.edu/content/2019/12/10/morning-blue-light-and-afternoon-red-light-increases-alertness-office-workers); [Buildings 2023 review](https://www.doi.org/10.3390/buildings13020471)
- **Bedroom light.** One night of sleeping in 100 lux raised nighttime heart rate, lowered HRV and raised next-morning insulin resistance — [Mason 2022, PNAS](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35286195) (from prior notes)

### Inferences
- **Evening (home 17:00, bed about 21:30):**
  - Outdoor daylight before about 18:30 is fine and may help mood. Grade: **Weak**.
  - From about 19:00, dim the house lights and use warm lamps. Grade: **Moderate–Strong**.
  - Phones and TV are acceptable if the brightness is low and the content is calming. A strict "no screens" rule isn't justified for adults and hurts adherence. Grade: **Weak–Moderate**.
  - Keep the bedroom dark (blackout curtains or an eye mask). Grade: **Moderate**.
- **Morning before a 12:00 standby:** get outdoor daylight soon after waking (e.g., 15–30 min walk or breakfast outside). It is low-risk, supports alertness and mood, and in a person on early starts it pushes the clock earlier, which is consistent with early rosters. Grade: **Moderate** for direction (PRC), **Weak** for the size of the next-day benefit.
  - Caveat: if the next duties are late or night duties, strong early-morning light is the wrong direction. The app must condition this on the upcoming roster (see the light section of prior notes).
- **Wording:** "Dim lights in the last couple of hours; it's the room light as much as the phone." Avoid claiming blue-light filters fix the problem. Their efficacy wasn't reviewed here, but prior consensus targets total melanopic light.

### Gaps
- The 2024 "pooled effect 0.28" screen meta-analysis could not be identified, so it is not used.
- No field data on screen use the evening after an early duty in shift workers.
- Blue-light-filter efficacy was not reviewed in this pass.
- No quantified next-day alertness benefit of 15–30 min morning daylight in sleep-restricted adults.

## 5. Social and personal time, wellbeing, and adherence (overly strict plans)

### Takeaway
Pleasurable leisure, time with family and friends, and psychological detachment from work are associated with less fatigue and better recovery in shift workers. Exhaustion erodes detachment, especially without enjoyable leisure. Shift workers follow advice that fits real life and family; rigid or one-ideal schedules lose users. The app should protect sleep while explicitly making room for social time, e.g., "an early dinner with friends is good; aim to be home and winding down by X". Grade: **Weak–Moderate** (observational recovery research plus app-adherence studies). I found no trial showing that strict plans directly reduce sleep outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **Sonnentag et al. 2014 (prospective, 109 employees).** Exhaustion predicted reduced psychological detachment over 4 weeks. The decline was strongest under time pressure and among employees **without pleasurable leisure experiences** — [Uni Mannheim record](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/48957)
- **ED nurses on shifts (n=166, Recovery Experience Questionnaire).** Better detachment was associated with less fatigue and better physical and mental health. Exercise, time with family and friends, and leisure pursuits were the strategies nurses used to reduce fatigue — summary via search; [Work journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/WOR-172631) (the attribution of this and the offshore study between the two links isn't fully clear from the search summary; verify)
- **Offshore shift workers (n=61 men, 14 days after a tour).** They actively pursued social, volunteer and leisure physical activities, which the authors judged likely to promote recovery — [Work 2017](https://content.iospress.com/articles/work/wor2631)
- **Japanese employees (n=2,520, 17.7% shift workers).** Family and friend support facilitated psychological detachment — [J-NIOSH Ind Health](https://www.jniosh.johas.go.jp/en/indu_hel/doc/IH_52_2_141.pdf)
- **Polish male shift workers (n=700).** Shift work lowered quality of life mainly via family life: lack of contact and irregular family meals — [agro.icm.edu.pl PDF](https://agro.icm.edu.pl/agro/element/bwmeta1.element.agro-af3791fb-4071-4901-9393-833d12d60519/c/fulltext730.pdf)
- **Adherence (from prior notes, `psychology_behaviour_trust.md`).**
  - Shift workers follow advice that is roster-personalised and flexible. Generic sleep-hygiene education doesn't change behaviour. Family obligations override sleep, with guilt about sleeping during family hours.
  - SleepSync gave "biologically viable recommendations… that account for work and social obligations"; 82% found it easy to integrate — [Murray 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10064476/)
  - The orthosomnia (rigid sleep perfectionism) scale has a "rigidity" factor — [Bergen Orthosomnia Scale, Front Sleep 2025, per prior notes]

### Inferences
- **Scenario:**
  - Frame the evening as "rest and things you enjoy", not "nothing but sleep prep". Suggest a social plan if wanted: early, low-alcohol, with food, ending about 1–1.5 h before bed. Grade: **Weak–Moderate**.
  - A short catch-up with family or a call is a good fit for the hour after getting home. Grade: **Weak**.
- **Avoid long checklists and rules.** Give one or two clear priorities (bedtime target and wind-down time) and let the rest flex. Grade: **Moderate** (adherence evidence).
- Social jetlag and family time in crew are covered in other notes. This pass adds no new data.

### Gaps
- No RCT showing that flexible vs strict sleep plans change sleep or adherence in shift workers.
- No study on how social evenings after early duties affect the next sleep.
- The nurse and offshore study attributions come from a search summary and need checking against the primary papers.
